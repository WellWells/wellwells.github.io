Looking Like You Know AI and Actually Knowing AI Are Two Different Things

Since AI landed in the workplace, the gap between sounding capable and actually being capable has gotten wider. From authority built purely out of jargon, to looking down on coworkers after barely learning AI, to making AI usage a KPI, to burning tokens on an Agent for something an API call could handle, here are a few patterns worth noticing, and why real judgment is knowing when not to use AI.

Foreword

Since AI showed up at work, the gap between sounding like you know what you're doing and actually knowing it has gotten wider. It used to take some effort to fake technical authority in a meeting: you had to at least memorize the jargon and walk through the process once. Now you just need to drop a few acronyms and the posture is already there.

Posture doesn't mean output. Here are a few patterns I keep running into at work. Building authority out of jargon and building superiority out of AI use are different tricks, but they run on the same logic underneath: strike the pose first, worry about delivering later.

Most of the stories below are secondhand, and what they point to is a pattern that turns up all over the place, so treat them as reference more than record. If a particular line happens to sting, a lot of the time someone ends up playing a part like this because somebody above them demanded it, or because performing was never optional to begin with.

Is jargon a moat or a fig leaf

There's a type of person who, whenever technical things come up, every so often drops in some phrase that sounds heavy and has little to do with what's actually being discussed, and the room pauses for a beat, not quite able to say what it has to do with anything. Really push him to explain something and he can't produce anything coherent. It reads less like an attempt to explain something clearly and more like a reminder that this person knows more than what's on the surface.

The quiet, competent type who can do the work but can't talk about it is a different story. This one is the reverse: all talk, no execution, and even the direction he's pointing at is usually wrong. The trickier part shows up when a decision actually needs to be made and someone has to own the outcome: that's exactly when this person is first in line to say it can't be done. The spec is badly written, the requirements are unclear. It sounds like quality control. What it actually does is drag expectations down in advance, so whatever gets delivered later clears the bar, and if it does blow up, there's already a ready-made excuse.

Sometimes this person even cuts into a serious discussion just to laugh at how naive it sounds. The discussion itself is fine. The laugh is just a way for him to position himself as the amused bystander looking down from somewhere higher.

This kind of persona is cheap to maintain. As long as the vocabulary is new and dense enough, most people won't have time to verify it and will just hand over the authority by default. But the moment there's something to actually deliver, the vocabulary stops holding up, because something that can't be built doesn't become real no matter how well it's described.

Barely learning AI and already looking down on everyone else

More common is someone who's only just started using AI and already feels like they've seen something nobody else has. Their output does improve, noticeably, but that improvement gets blown up into an identity. They start looking down on coworkers still doing things the old way, calling them inefficient, behind the times.

This is the Dunning-Kruger effect. Early on in any skill, right when your ability is thinnest, you're the most likely to overrate yourself, because you don't yet know enough to see how much you don't know. It usually takes going deeper to realize how cheap that early confidence was. A lot of people who just picked up AI are stuck at exactly this stage, mistaking it for talent and forgetting it's a phase everyone eventually moves past.

That confidence eventually grows into some alarming claims. The most extreme one I've heard is that AI-generated code doesn't need to be read, since it runs anyway. People who say this usually have no idea what they're doing, and think the whole job can just be handed over to AI. But code that runs and code that is correct were never the same thing, and seeing the gap between them takes a certain level of skill. The people who can't see it are, conveniently, the same people most willing to say the line.

That rush isn't imaginary. The moment AI hands back a response it triggers a dopamine hit, running on roughly the same mechanism as a slot machine. You never quite know if this round is going to land a genuinely good answer, and that uncertainty is exactly what keeps you pulling the lever again. The rush is real. It just isn't the same thing as actual output at work.

But pay has never worked that way. The senior coworker next door who won't touch AI and does everything the old-fashioned way might still be earning more, because pay has always tracked tenure, position, and leverage in negotiation, and how well you write a prompt doesn't make that list. Treating AI fluency as proof of rising status gets the causality backwards. What's even more ironic is that their monthly salary alone could cover at least five Claude Max 20x subscriptions, and what they ship isn't necessarily less than what you ship. What's actually raising your value is probably those subscriptions.

And a lot of what this person says is stuff you could find with a quick search anyway. Say it enough times and you start believing it's your own firsthand insight. These two types aren't mutually exclusive either. More often than not, the same person carries both, just in different proportions.

How do you know you're flying the shortest path

A while back I asked AI something simple: what's the shortest route from Taiwan to San Francisco. The answer came back beautifully. Great circle route, jet stream, passing south of the Aleutians, about 10,400 km, roughly eleven hours eastbound with a tailwind, every term you'd expect. So I followed up: why not fly left instead, straight west. West gets you to San Francisco too, of course. It just means going around the other side of the planet, thirty thousand kilometers instead of ten.

That's exactly where it gets hard: flying west does get you there. Almost every heading gets you there in the end. The long way around gets you there too, burning three times the fuel and circling most of the planet along the way, and the whole time, the instruments faithfully show that you're making progress. So the real question was never whether you'll arrive. It's how you know the route you're on is the shortest one and not a detour. AI can't answer that for you, because every route it hands you sounds equally sure of itself.

And if the captain has no judgment of his own, the whole thing inverts: AI says it can be done, so you think it can; it says it can't, so you don't try. On paper you're still the one setting the heading. In practice, AI is. The blind spot cuts both ways, too. In one direction, it describes a detour with total confidence and calls it the shortest path. In the other, an authoritative source tells you plainly that something can't be done, and because you don't know enough, you simply believe it. People who don't know will never spot the error, because they don't know they should be suspicious in the first place.

I ran into the second kind recently. There was a newer revision of a hardware platform, and a capability we wanted to use, so I threw the question at AI. It searched around, dug up the vendor's support page, and answered without hesitation: not supported, and sure enough, nothing in the official samples either. What AI does at this stage is basically search and skim docs, laying whatever facts turn up in front of you. The facts themselves aren't wrong; they just stop wherever the search stopped. If your own judgment isn't strong enough, it's easy to take not supported as the final word and call it closed. But the hardware was perfectly capable of it. The vendor simply hadn't packaged it up and handed it over ready-made, and if you know the low-level side well enough, you can work around the official samples, put the pieces together yourself, and get the result you were after. What the vendor said was we don't provide this, not this isn't possible, and those two sentences are very far apart. Telling them apart comes down to how deeply you understand the layer underneath, not how thoroughly AI searched. So whether a heading is right often can't be looked up at all. It comes down to whether you believe the thing can be done, and whether you know how to do it.

Writing code is where the consequences show up fastest. Give the same tool to two people, and one uses it to build an architecture they've already thought through, while the other uses it to mass-produce something they haven't. Same tool, and what separates them is how well the person understood the problem to begin with. That line from earlier, the one about AI-generated code not needing to be read, is exactly this heading flown all the way out. The system rots quickly, in the places you can't see, and it all comes due at once the moment someone has to take it over, change it, or debug it in production, usually far enough in that the whole thing has to be scrapped and rebuilt.

A copilot can hold the stick and run the checklist, and do both faster and more steadily than you would. But the captain sets the heading, and the captain is the one who answers for it. If you need the copilot to tell you whether to go east or west, you're gambling. On a flattened map, the line that looks straightest is often not the shortest, and the most convenient way to use AI frequently isn't either.

When AI usage becomes a KPI it turns into a performance

Once a workplace like this gets measured by a KPI, the whole thing goes off the rails. The root of the problem is usually that the person setting the KPI doesn't understand the technology. They have no way to judge whether a team is producing better work because of AI, since that's hard to measure and would require actually understanding the work. Usage, on the other hand, is a number they can read and grade. To put it plainly, making AI usage a KPI is a genuinely stupid idea. It measures input, not output. When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

And the moment a number becomes the target, people below start optimizing for that number. The fastest way to drive usage up is to mass-produce things that don't mean anything, since nobody is going back to check whether any of that output was useful. As long as tokens burn and the counter moves, the number looks good.

The funniest part is watching the most expensive model get pointed at the most trivial work. Switching a git branch is a command of about fifteen characters that finishes in a fraction of a second. But to keep the usage figure healthy, it apparently deserves a fresh conversation, a full description of the current state of the repo, several seconds of Claude Opus 4.8 thinking it over, and finally, with great ceremony, executing the line git checkout -b behind the scenes to cut the branch. A frontier model you wouldn't be embarrassed to point at olympiad math problems, sent to cut a branch for someone who probably doesn't even know git. Either way, this week's usage rate ticks up another notch.

There's a more direct version of the game: deliberately blow through the quota. Claude meters usage in five-hour windows, so if you make a point of maxing one out, everything cools down and nothing works until it resets. Quota gone, no more work getting done, and the usage line still climbs beautifully.

Anyone with real fundamentals watching this doesn't think here's someone who understands AI. They think here's someone who doesn't know what he's doing. But the manager can't see that difference. The only signal available to him is who looks the most enthusiastic about AI, so the best performer becomes, in his eyes, the employee most in step with the times. When the people above can't read the work, the people below learn exactly who they're performing for, and that's usually how the good gets driven out by the bad. Perform it long enough and you start pulling others into the act, leaning over while a coworker is deep in something to ask why they haven't just asked AI.

There are still plenty of companies like this, whole organizations living inside a kind of inflated expectation, as if the mere mention of AI made a decision automatically more correct, more forward-looking. What you end up with is an environment where whether you used AI matters more than whether you got a result. The KPI wants a good-looking usage number, and a good-looking usage number is exactly what it gets, along with the pile of garbage sitting underneath it that nobody wants to look at.

When AI slop gets packaged up and sold

Everything so far has stayed inside the company. Garbage produced to feed a KPI at least rots at home. What's more striking is that the same kind of thing now gets packaged into a product, stamped with an alarming price, and sold on the open market.

There's a term for this online: AI slop, the low-quality, AI-mass-produced content nobody really asked for. It used to mean articles and images. Now entire companies and entire service catalogs get churned out the same way. I came across a textbook example: a company pitching itself as an AI-driven full-stack shop, its homepage lined with a whole row of AI departments, each with its own Agent, staffed from engineering all the way down to legal and finance. The portfolio listed dozens of projects spanning wildly unrelated fields, from medical to the occult to virtual showrooms. The tech stack list was just as dense, too many to check one by one.

But the worst part of this kind of thing is how dangerous it is. That flagship custom AI Agent, once you strip it down, is a large language model wired to a backend with no defenses at all. I've seen one get broken open: a few lines were enough to make it drop its assigned role and start using the whole host as its own shell, and what came back was genuinely real, not the model playing along. From there the privileges climbed all the way to root, until even the Claude credentials file on the host could be read.

Their homepage even carries a line boasting AI cost: TWD 0. A key that pays someone else's bill sits wide open for anyone who cares to reach for it, so no wonder they can put TWD 0 on the site: the money it burns was never theirs. A publicly exposed chatbot, open to prompt injection, sitting on top of root access and a plaintext key, is exactly what's being sold here for TWD 12,000.

That lays the whole point of this piece out in the open. It looks like it has everything, a row of departments, dozens of projects, the newest frameworks, but whoever built it has no idea what they wired to what. The posture is maxed out, and underneath it is something that answers whatever you ask and hands you the key on the way out. Seeing technical judgment go missing to this degree, and still get packaged and sold as a product, is the part that actually unsettles me.

Look at how other people did it before you start

All of it comes back to the same gap in judgment: not knowing when AI should be used, and not knowing when it shouldn't.

In academic research the first step is the literature review, not the writing: what has been done, how far it got, where it got stuck. Only once that's clear do you get to say what you intend to improve and what problem you're actually solving. Engineering is the same. Look at how other people implemented the same thing, what they ran into, why they settled on the path they settled on, and then start. It's usually faster than reasoning it out from zero, and steadier. With someone else's road to work from, you at least know where you're standing.

But a lot of people skip that step entirely: throw one sentence at AI and it hands back a complete-looking architecture, a workflow, a plan, nicely formatted with clearly numbered steps. The only problem is that none of it has been verified. Looking like it runs is not the same as holding up. Confirming whether it holds up still means going out to check, to try, to hit the walls yourself, and there is no shortcut through that part. It saves you the typing. It doesn't save you a single one of the holes you were always going to fall into.

That step has a prerequisite: you need a foundation first. Without one, you have no way to judge whether what other people are saying is right or wrong, and reading more sources just means copying. Copy something wrong, and you won't notice that either.

The principle is to lock down the mechanical, repeatable steps first, and only hand the part that genuinely needs judgment to AI. That's what makes a workflow fast, keeps token usage from exploding, and makes the result predictable. Flip that around, and using an Agent to click through a computer screen step by step, to do something an API call or an MCP integration could handle reliably, is the same waste as pointing a flagship model at a git branch earlier. The task gets done either way, at several times the time and token cost, for a result that isn't necessarily more reliable.

Making that call usually isn't the outcome of weighing real costs. It's that using AI to do the thing has become more important than getting the thing done. Same as the jargon-fortress type from earlier: the posture comes ahead of the output.

Conclusion

Telling whether someone actually knows their stuff comes down to something simple: have them explain it to someone with zero background, then ask a few follow-up questions. People who really understand something can usually get to the point in a couple of sentences, and they'll say plainly when they're not sure. People running on persona alone either start circling or start getting irritated the moment you push for detail.

Not knowing something is fine. Nobody starts out knowing. The trouble starts when you don't know, decide to act like you do, and lean on the fact that nobody nearby knows either, using that to carry the moment on sheer confidence. That trick works once, maybe twice. It doesn't survive a third. That type from earlier, always first to say it can't be done: honestly, hand the same task to AI, and it might do a better job than you would.

That's exactly why a foundation can't be hidden. AI can produce something that looks complete for you. What it cannot produce is your understanding. The work can be its work, but when the question comes, you're the one standing there. And in the second you can't answer it, not one of the terms you stacked up or the usage numbers you drove up is going to save you.

And fundamentals hide in small places. Git is a good example. It isn't as hard as people make it out to be, since ninety percent of daily use is just commit, but some people take one look at a command line and back away, and others assume the command line is the only way to use git, unaware that graphical clients and extensions are sitting right there. The tool was never the problem. Whether you're willing to spend one afternoon getting comfortable with it is the problem.

The most extreme case I've heard was someone who thought git was a Claude thing.

Then there's treating not using an IDE as a badge. People argue that writing code doesn't need an IDE at all, that a terminal with AI running in it is enough, as if opening one fewer window automatically leveled you up. But jump-to-definition, a debugger, and a diff view exist precisely to help you understand code, and the people who throw all of that away are, as a rule, the same people who weren't reading the code in the first place. This isn't where AI can help. It has nothing to do with knowledge and everything to do with whether you've ever actually done the work.

As for what to do about it yourself, I keep landing on the same short list: talk less, do more, and when it's time to draw a conclusion, bring concrete evidence for it. The people who actually get value out of AI tend not to talk about it much, because the time it saved them went into doing something else.

I'll probably keep sorting it out the same blunt way I always have: not by who talks the best game, but by who actually has something built.
