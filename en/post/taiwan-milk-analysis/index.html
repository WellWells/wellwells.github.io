Introduction

On January 1, 2025, Taiwan dropped its tariff on liquid milk from New Zealand at the end of the 12-year phase-out under the 2013 ANZTEC agreement. I thought milk would finally get cheaper. A few weeks later, I found the same carton in the dairy aisle at PX Mart. It was still TWD 85.

In Numbeo's 2025 figures, milk in Taiwan averaged USD 3.20 (about TWD 100) per liter, the highest price in Asia. A dairy farmer received about TWD 30 per liter. By the time the milk reached a supermarket, it sold for TWD 85 to 120, with 30–40% of the shelf price going to the retailer.

I wanted to know how much of that TWD 85 reached the farm, and what made the "fresh milk" in the fridge different from the long-life milk on the shelf.

Milk Sterilization Methods and Nutrient Changes

How Milk is Sterilized: Key Methods and Differences

The heat treatment method decides how long the milk keeps. Four are common on the market:

With LTLT (low-temperature, long-time pasteurization), the dairy holds raw milk at 63–65 °C for 30 minutes. The heat kills most pathogens, though some heat-tolerant bacteria survive. The milk keeps for about 5 to 7 days in the refrigerator. Small dairies and some cheesemakers use this method.

HTST (high-temperature, short-time pasteurization) is common in the US. Milk flows through heated pipes at 72 °C for 15 seconds and retains much of its fresh flavor. Refrigerated, it keeps for about 7 to 12 days in Taiwan and up to three weeks in the US.

Extended Shelf Life (ESL) This sits right between pasteurization and sterilization, heating milk to 120–135 °C for a few seconds. Since it's not fully sterile, it must be chilled immediately.

  • In Japan, they heat it to 120–130 °C for a couple of seconds to get a 10 to 14-day shelf life.
  • In the US, it's called Ultra-Pasteurized (UP), and they heat it to at least 138 °C. That stretches the refrigerated life to 30 to 90 days.
  • In Taiwan, a TFDA rule that took effect in July 2025 lets refrigerated milk be labeled "ESL Fresh Milk" if it lasts over 30 days and meets high-hygiene standards.

Ultra High Temperature (UHT) The goal here is commercial sterility. The milk goes to 135–150 °C for a few seconds and is sealed in aseptic packs, which makes it shelf-stable at room temperature for months.

  • In Japan, they package UHT milk in aluminum-lined cartons to keep out light and oxygen.
  • In Taiwan, we call this long-life milk (保久乳), and it sits on the normal supermarket shelves.

Sorting Out Labeling and Regional Rules

LTLT and HTST are both forms of pasteurization. In Taiwan, a carton marked "UHT Fresh Milk" may contain milk heated to around 130 °C. You'll find it in the refrigerated section, and it needs to stay cold.

Japan groups milk by storage conditions. The label tells you whether an unopened carton needs refrigeration or can be kept at room temperature. There is no separate ESL category.

How the Rules Differ Internationally

Most of the "fresh milk" we buy in Taiwan is processed using high heat, but different countries define these terms in their own ways.

For instance, international guidelines (from Codex and the IDF) say UHT milk must be heated to 135–150 °C for 2 to 4 seconds to kill all bacteria. Japan, on the other hand, defines UHT as heating to 120–150 °C for 1–3 seconds.

ESL milk is typically heated to 120–135 °C for 3 to 5 seconds. In Japan, standard pasteurized milk only has a 7-day shelf life, but if you heat it to 120 °C, they let you label it for 14 days. Several big Taiwanese dairies actually run their lines at 130 °C for 3–5 seconds. US dairies use higher temperatures and fill cartons under highly sanitary conditions. The milk still needs refrigeration.

Across these heat treatments, protein, fat and carbohydrate content change very little, as does the calorie count.

What Happens to the Nutrients?

The table shows little change in casein, milk fat, lactose or calcium after heat treatment.

Heat changes the structure of some whey proteins. The milk still contains the same total amount of protein, which the digestive system breaks down into amino acids.

The table also shows losses of vitamins B₁, B₂, B₆ and B₁₂. HTST generally preserves more of these vitamins than UHT. Folate shows the largest loss in the table, about 30–40% after UHT treatment.

Supply Chain Roles and Processes

Dairy Farmers and Livestock Operations

Farmers chill raw milk in tanks on the farm and keep it below 10 °C until collection. A dairy tanker comes daily or every other day. The dairy checks each batch and can pay a lower price or reject milk that fails inspection.

Most of our milk comes from a few main hubs: Liuying in Tainan, Lunbei in Yunlin, Wandan in Pingtung, Fubao in Changhua, Chulu in Taitung, Ruisui in Hualien, and Tongxiao in Miaoli.

The Big Processing Plants

Processors buy raw milk using seasonal price tiers (usually winter, spring/autumn, and summer) because cows produce less milk when it's hot. The Raw Milk Price Review Committee negotiates these baseline prices. Since 1997, plants also factor in milk safety markers (like bacteria levels) alongside traditional fat and solids content.

Once raw milk arrives at the plant, it goes through a standard check for sediment and bacteria, gets its fat levels standardized, is heated (either pasteurized or UHT-treated), and is finally packaged in a sterile room.

The processor's share of the shelf price also covers packaging, quality testing, marketing and some of the transport. Uni-President, Wei Chuan and Kuang Chuan are among the processors in Taiwan.

Supermarkets and Retailers

Once the cartons are filled, they're shipped out in refrigerated trucks to supermarkets, hypermarkets, convenience stores, cafes, and bakeries.

Part of the price on the shelf at PX Mart pays for refrigeration, warehouse space, rent and staff. The retailer also keeps a margin, and dairies pay fees to get their products onto store shelves.

Common Questions About Milk

Is long-life milk less nutritious than fresh milk?

I used to think preservatives were what made long-life milk keep for months. It keeps because of the heat treatment and aseptic packaging. The heat changes the structure of some whey proteins and reduces levels of vitamins such as folate and B₁₂. The texture can differ, but a glass of long-life milk provides about as much protein and calcium as a glass of fresh milk.

Why does Taiwanese fresh milk taste richer and sweeter than imported milk?

Taiwanese fresh milk tastes sweeter and richer because about 90% of it is heated to around 130 °C. This high heat causes a Maillard reaction—basically caramelizing the milk sugars slightly—giving it that cooked, sweet aroma. Standard milk in the US or Europe is usually pasteurized at lower temperatures, so it tastes much lighter. Also, Taiwanese brands tend to standardize their milk to have a higher fat content, which makes it feel thicker in your mouth.

What is the deal with the "Fresh Milk" label on imported milk?

Right now, imported milk (like Costco's Kirkland Signature) is allowed to carry the "Fresh Milk" label as long as it stays cold during shipping. But starting in July 2026, the government is tightening the rules. Only milk produced in Taiwan can use the "Fresh Milk" name. Imported milk will have to be labeled as something else, like "long-life" or "ESL" milk, depending on how it was processed.

What kind of milk is in my bubble tea or latte?

A shop may use powdered creamer or reconstituted milk in a milk tea. A drink sold as "fresh milk tea" usually contains fresh milk. The word latte just means milk in Italian. To find out what the shop uses, look for a description such as "Taiwanese fresh milk" or "long-life milk from New Zealand." In 2025, Taiwan piloted a voluntary program encouraging chains to give customers that information.

Most imported milk comes in as ambient long-life milk, refrigerated ESL milk, or frozen milk blocks. Processing plants thaw those frozen blocks, add water to reconstitute them, and sell them to coffee shops as "Barista Milk" because it froths better.

My Takeaways

The word "low-temperature" on Ruisui's premium milk caught my eye. The figures in the price table tell me more. Ruisui heats the milk to 72 °C for 15 seconds. Hsiukuluan uses LTLT for its fresh milk, heating it to 65 °C for 30 minutes.

I'd like to see the heating method, time and cold-storage conditions printed on the carton. "Fresh milk" alone doesn't tell me whether the dairy heated the milk to 72 °C or 130 °C. "Taiwan Fresh Milk" or "Domestic Fresh Milk" would help distinguish local milk from imports. Local long-life milk could carry its place of origin too. If the milk came from Ruisui or Lunbei, I'd like to be able to see that when I buy it.

For imported milk, I also look at the temperature and shelf life on the carton. The Kirkland carton in the table lists 138 °C for 2 seconds and a refrigerated shelf life of 85 days. The Hokkaido Tokachi Yotsuba milk lists 120 °C for 2 seconds and 35 days. Both are imports, but one keeps for over a month longer.

How much of my TWD 85 went back to the farm? The estimates above put the retailer's share at 35–40% and the farmer's at around 30%. I'd like the carton to show what the dairy paid for the milk and how much of the price went to processing and retail. On an imported carton of long-life milk packed overseas, that would also tell me whether any of the purchase price went to a Taiwanese farm or processor.

The school milk program

According to the Ministry of Education, 863 schools across seven counties ordered 2.42 million bottles under the "Fresh Milk in Every Class" program. Long-life milk accounted for 1.87 million bottles, about 77% of the order. The remaining 550,000 were fresh milk.

If children don't drink fresh milk when it arrives, the school needs fridge space for it. Rural schools also need refrigerated deliveries. A school with limited fridge space can store unopened, single-serving cartons of long-life milk at room temperature. Each serving provides about as much calcium and protein as fresh milk.

Alongside the 14-day limit for an unopened carton, I'd like the standard to consider quality after opening. One possible test would measure whether the milk retains its flavor and quality after seven days at 0–7 °C. I'd include domestic long-life milk in the "fresh milk" category if it passed that test.
