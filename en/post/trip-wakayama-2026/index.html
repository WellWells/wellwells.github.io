Kii Peninsula Road Trip: 9-Day Itinerary and Costs

Our nine-day drive from Wakayama through Shirahama, Nachi Falls and Ise-Shima, with rental car tips, hotel stays and recorded costs for two.

In late October 2026, my friend and I took a nine-day road trip around Japan's Kii Peninsula to celebrate my friend's birthday. We flew from Kaohsiung to Kansai Airport on October 24 and picked up our rental car in Wakayama the next day. We followed the coast through Shirahama and around Cape Shionomisaki, then continued through Nachikatsuura and Kumano to Ise-Shima. After visiting Yokkaichi and Iga, we returned to Kansai Airport and flew home to Taiwan on November 1.

A typhoon approached Taiwan the day before we left, followed by heavy rain and car trouble on our second day. From then on, our trip differed from the original plan. This post records our actual route and which stops I thought were worth visiting.

We flew with China Airlines in both directions, using Terminal 1 at Kansai Airport.

Day 1: Kaohsiung to Kansai

Typhoon Champi was approaching Taiwan on Friday, October 23, and work and classes were canceled in Kaohsiung. Our flight was the next day. The typhoon was still nearby, but the weather had improved a little, and CI176 left as planned.

We arrived at Kaohsiung Airport at 1 p.m. After passing through security and passport control, I went to the Southern Premium Lounge (南苑國際商務中心), while my friend went to the China Airlines lounge. This was my second visit to the Southern Premium Lounge, and I spent a long time eating and drinking there.

Our flight was on China Airlines' Pikachu Jet. We took off on time after 3 p.m. and encountered some light turbulence. By the time we cleared immigration and customs and reached the shops in the arrivals area, it was nearly 8 p.m.

We then took JR West trains to Wakayama, changing at Hineno Station. The one-way fare was JPY 880. I developed diarrhea that evening and suspect I had eaten and drunk too much in the lounge at midday. From arriving at Kaohsiung Airport at 1 p.m. to reaching Comfort Hotel Wakayama at 10 p.m., we spent almost the entire first day in transit.

Reserve an ETC Card in Advance

We couldn't rent an electronic toll collection (ETC) card on this trip. Cards are hard to get at the rental counter without a reservation, so book one in advance. TOYOTA Rent a Car's Taiwan booking system lets you reserve a card. Its Japanese booking system offers discounts and lower prices, but you need to confirm card availability by email or phone because you cannot reserve one online. Some expressway exits, including smart interchanges, are restricted to vehicles equipped for ETC. Without a card, using the expressways is less convenient, and you may not be able to use your intended exit.

Kuroshio Market and Kinokawa Persimmons

It was pouring when we picked up the car at 9:30 a.m., and the sky was very dark. We left around 10 a.m., stopped first at the UNIQLO Kokutai Road branch to collect clothes and pants we had ordered online, and reached Kuroshio Market at Wakayama Marina City at 11:30 a.m.

We weren't very hungry, so we had a simple lunch of seafood rice bowls. At 12:30 p.m. we watched the tuna-cutting show. Staff cut up a whole tuna in front of the audience in about 10 to 15 minutes. Shows are held at 12:30 p.m. and 3 p.m. on weekdays, with an additional 11 a.m. show on weekends.

After the show, we bought fruit at the neighboring Kinokuni Fruit Village (紀ノ国フルーツ村). Kinokawa persimmons (紀の川柿) cost just JPY 300 each. I would expect this variety to be fairly expensive in Taiwan. The flesh had dark brown specks and was very sweet. They were delicious.

All the cash we had exchanged before leaving Taiwan was in JPY 10,000 notes. When we were ready to leave Kuroshio Market, we discovered that the parking payment machine accepted only coins and notes of JPY 1,000 or less. The parking fee was JPY 1,000, and we had no small notes, so we had to run through heavy rain to a convenience store to get change. Many automated payment machines in Japan do not accept JPY 10,000 notes. It is best to break large notes before heading out and carry a few JPY 1,000 notes.

Wakayama Castle and the Free Zoo

Wakayama Castle was the seat of the Kishu Tokugawa family. The current main tower was rebuilt after World War II.

We arrived at 2:10 p.m., and admission was JPY 410 per person. It was still raining, and we had to climb a flight of stone steps to enter the main tower. Inside, the information panels were all in Japanese and focused mainly on the history of the Kishu Tokugawa family. The internet connection was poor, so uploading photos for AI translation was too slow to be useful. I understood very little of the text, and the visit did not leave much of an impression.

Wakayama Castle Park Zoo nearby was more enjoyable. It is run by Wakayama City and admission is free. The animals include penguins and meerkats, and I took a close-up photo of a meerkat.

Staying in Shirahama: Hotel Sanrakuso

After visiting a local supermarket, we took the expressway toward Shirahama. As we left the expressway, we heard a noise from under the car and drove straight to a TOYOTA rental branch. Staff arranged a new car as a replacement, but the exchange took at least 40 minutes. We could only wait at the branch, which felt like wasted time.

We were staying at Hotel Sanrakuso, where dinner was served in our room, so the delay made us anxious about arriving in time. We called the hotel and reached a staff member from China who was working in Japan. They assured us that dinner would be fine as long as we arrived within the required time. We made it in time for the meal.

Our room had tatami mat flooring, and dinner was a generous spread. We had no other plans that evening. After dinner, I used my computer for a while and later went to the hot spring baths. Shirahama is known as one of Japan's three ancient hot spring resorts, along with Arima and Dogo.

Sandanbeki and Senjojiki

We had planned to visit Sandanbeki and Senjojiki the previous afternoon but did not make it, so we moved them to this morning.

Sandanbeki is a stretch of coastal cliffs in Shirahama. An elevator in the building beside the cliffs takes visitors 36 m down into a sea cave. The official website lists adult admission at JPY 1,500. Inside is a shrine dedicated to Muro Daibenzaiten. Staff take your photo and then ask whether you would like to buy it.

The waves crashing into the cave are spectacular and are the main attraction, followed by Shiofuki-iwa, the blowhole rock. We took a brief look at the shrine and spent less than half an hour in the cave altogether.

A little after 10 a.m., we visited nearby Senjojiki, a broad sandstone formation sloping toward the sea. It reminded me of Xiaoyeliu, a coastal rock formation in Taitung, Taiwan. The rocks were beautiful, and we spent some time exploring and taking photos.

Toretore Seafood Market in Shirahama

At Fukubishi (福菱), we bought Kagerou (かげろう) sweets as gifts to take home and ate the other sweets we bought there right away. We then visited Toretore Market (とれとれ市場), which sells fresh fish, prepared seafood dishes, and souvenirs. It even has claw machines. I thought the market was impressive. There were plenty of tour buses, so it seems to be a regular stop for tour groups.

We bought a box of five slices of bluefin tuna otoro (大トロ), the premium fatty cut, for JPY 2,400. That works out to JPY 480, or roughly TWD 100, per slice. I remember the tuna being a little stringy. It tasted good, but I didn't find otoro as special as I had expected.

Owashi Farm

We continued south from Shirahama and reached the Owashi Farm shop around 1:30 p.m. for soft serve and milk. My friend had found the shop before the trip. It is easy to miss along National Route 42, and you need to ring the bell for a staff member to come out.

I ordered a large mixed-flavor soft serve. It tasted slightly salty, and I thought it was average. The portion was large, and I ate slowly, so it kept melting before I could finish it. I didn't enjoy that. If I ordered again, I would choose plain milk flavor.

Cape Shionomisaki Lighthouse and the Southernmost Point of Honshu

Our next stop was Cape Shionomisaki Lighthouse, where adult admission was JPY 300. I did not find the lighthouse itself particularly special. The stairs were more memorable: after a 68-step spiral staircase, the final section is almost vertical, and you need to hold on as you climb. This was also where I learned that many Japanese lighthouses are open to visitors.

Near the lighthouse is the stone monument marking the southernmost point of Honshu.

While I was there, I learned that many young people from the Kushimoto area had moved to Thursday Island in Australia to dive for silver-lipped pearl oysters, whose shells mainly went into high-end buttons. The Shiokaze Rest House displays diving helmets and suits. Shiba Ryotaro's Mokuyojima no Yakai (木曜島の夜会) is about these divers.

Hashigui-iwa

Hashigui-iwa is a row of rock pillars standing in the sea. At low tide there is a path out among them, but the tide may already have been in when we arrived, and it had started to rain again.

Michi-no-Eki Kushimoto Hashigui-iwa (道の駅くしもと橋杭岩), a roadside rest stop with souvenir shops, is right beside the rocks. We browsed the souvenirs after taking photos, but the stop did not leave much of an impression on me.

Cave Baths at Hotel Urashima

We reached Hotel Urashima's designated parking lot around 4:30 p.m. Hotel Urashima (ホテル浦島) has no parking beside the hotel itself. Drivers need to use a separate parking lot and transfer to a shuttle bus. Another option is to take the hotel's shuttle boat from Katsuura Port. We chose the more straightforward bus option. The driver asks whether you want to go to the port or directly to the hotel.

Dinner was a buffet with another tuna-cutting show. The food was good, and the setting was fine. One cabinet held 33 types of alcoholic drinks, with sake and beer available separately. Most of the bottles in the cabinet were flavored drinks, and only a few appealed to me.

We used Bokido (忘帰洞) that evening and Genbudo (玄武洞) the next morning. Both are hot spring baths inside natural caves, with the sea just outside. Listening to the waves while soaking felt much like being in an open-air bath. Looking back, I enjoyed the unusual setting.

The hotel has several wings, and the long walks between them were frustrating. Reaching the baths in another wing the next morning took seven minutes each way. Almost 15 minutes of walking just to use the baths felt excessive.

Nachi Falls and Kumano Nachi Taisha

The weather cleared on October 27. At 10 a.m., we visited Hiro Shrine (飛瀧神社) to see Nachi Falls. The 133 m waterfall is spectacular. The misty air and surrounding forest made it a refreshing place to spend time. A viewing platform farther up offers a closer view for an additional JPY 500, but we did not go up.

Hiro Shrine has a huge omikuji container used to draw fortunes. It is about 133 cm tall, echoing the height of the waterfall in meters. It was the largest I had ever seen. My friend drew a fortune, but I did not.

We then followed the uphill path past the three-story pagoda to Kumano Nachi Taisha. The sun was intense, with hardly a cloud in the sky. I had not brought an umbrella for shade, and it was too hot to keep my jacket on. The climb from the falls requires some stamina, and I complained quite a bit along the way.

We may have taken the wrong path. Later, we saw another route with neatly built stone steps, which I think was reached by continuing uphill from the parking lot. Looking back, the climb itself was not especially tiring; the main problem was the strong sun. The photos remind me of the discomfort, but I still think the climb was worthwhile. The scenery at the top was beautiful, and I took plenty of photos I liked.

There was a drum performance at the shrine, and we enjoyed watching for about 10 minutes. Shrines do not usually leave a strong impression on me, but I consider both Nachi Falls and Kumano Nachi Taisha essential stops. Both are free to visit and photograph beautifully.

Tuna Rice Bowl Lunch

At 12:20 p.m., we had lunch at a restaurant we had researched before the trip. We ordered a bluefin tuna rice bowl and a seared tuna rice bowl, priced at JPY 3,500 to JPY 3,800 each. Both were good.

Umigame Park, Shishi-iwa, and Onigajo

In the afternoon, we followed National Route 42 toward Kumano City. We stopped briefly at Michi-no-Eki Kihocho Umigame Park (道の駅 紀宝町ウミガメ公園), a roadside rest stop in Kiho Town that keeps sea turtles. Our next stop was Shishi-iwa (獅子岩, Lion Rock). Its outline resembles a lion's head, complete with eyes and teeth, but we finished taking photos in just three minutes.

We reached Onigajo (鬼ヶ城) at 2:50 p.m. The Chinese translation, 鬼城, means "Ghost Castle." I did not like the name, but the coastal rock formations were breathtaking. The path runs for about 1 km along cliffs filled with sea caves. Much of it has just a single railing between the path and the steep drop to the sea. It reminded me of exposed sections of the trail on Yushan, Taiwan's highest mountain, and felt quite thrilling.

We turned back at the ninth marked attraction along the path. Continuing would have meant walking toward the sun, and we were already tired. The round trip took about an hour. When we started at 3 p.m., parts of the path were shaded, but others were still very sunny. Arriving around 4 p.m. might be more comfortable.

Yakiniku and MOS Burger in Kumano

Hotel NAMI is close to Onigajo, and we liked the hotel. We returned there first to shower and rest. At 5:30 p.m., we ate at Nanki (なんき), a local restaurant serving yakiniku, Japanese-style grilled meat. Beef tongue and other cuts cost JPY 3,150 for two, or about TWD 320 each. The food was not particularly good, but it was inexpensive. We did not order much and were still hungry afterward. We then visited the Okuwa Kumano supermarket. By 8 p.m. we were hungry again, so we ate at MOS Burger and later had snacks at the hotel.

Ouchiyama Zoo

Heading north from Kumano, we first stopped at the roadside station Michi-no-Eki Kii-Nagashima Manbo (道の駅 紀伊長島マンボウ) for food to take back to Taiwan, then continued to Ouchiyama Zoo.

Adult admission to Ouchiyama Zoo was a fairly steep JPY 2,000, although no one seemed to be watching the entrance. I would not recommend making a special trip here.

The main problem was the fencing. The mesh was dense and set well back from the viewing areas, and some enclosures also had glass doors. Those may have been closed because of the wind. The zoo has lions, black bears, goats, chickens, and geese. The lions roared impressively, and the black bears looked imposing. The number of potentially dangerous animals may explain the heavy fencing, but it dominated every photo.

After leaving the zoo, we had a light meal nearby at Ouchiyama Milk Village (大内山ミルク村), which sells the full range of the local Ouchiyama Milk brand.

The Ama Divers' Hut

A little after 2 p.m., we visited the free Ama Culture Museum in Osatsu, Toba, to learn about the history of ama, traditional women divers. We then had a meal at Hachiman Kamado (海女小屋はちまんかまど), an ama hut, starting at 2:30 p.m. and finishing around 3:20 p.m.

Despite the high ratings, I strongly advise against this ama hut. We booked the 75-minute Plan C and paid TWD 2,443 for two. An ama grilled the seafood and served it to us. The interactive part of the experience involved dancing, and I took photos of my friend.

One dish was a sea snail, and I was unsure whether its internal organs were edible. I took a photo and asked AI to identify it. The response also mentioned that it could taste very bitter. I tried it, and it was intensely bitter. I ate quite a bit of fish afterward, but I did not enjoy the meal.

The worst problem was the flies. More than ten were flying around the food throughout the meal. Paying that much to eat with flies around us felt like a waste of money.

Yokoyama Observatory and Meoto Iwa

Just before 4 p.m. we reached Yokoyama Observatory in Shima City, which looks down on Ago Bay, its surface dotted with islands of all sizes. The weather was good that day and the view was clear, but the wind had become very strong and the temperature had dropped.

On the way back, we stopped at Meoto Iwa, the Wedded Rocks, in Futami. We arrived at 5 p.m. to strong winds, large waves, and plenty of visitors. I found it more interesting than Shishi-iwa, and the crashing waves made the evening visit more dramatic. About 15 to 30 minutes is enough here.

Nearby Futami Okitama Shrine has an association with frogs, and frog motifs are everywhere. The shrine itself did not leave much of an impression on me. A path along the seawall takes you close to the waves, reminding me of Gaomei Wetlands in Taiwan.

We had dinner at Saizeriya, which was inexpensive and good. I recommend it. At 7:30 p.m., we visited MEGA Don Quijote Ise Kamiji before returning to Route Inn Ise at 9 p.m.

Ise Grand Shrine

We visited only the Inner Shrine at Ise Grand Shrine and skipped the Outer Shrine. We wanted to see the main sights rather than pray. It was another hot day, but the many trees provided shade and made the grounds pleasant to explore.

After visiting the Inner Shrine, we went to the nearby shopping street for Akafuku mochi, a sweet rice cake covered in red bean paste. It was quite sweet. For lunch, we had Ise udon at Fukusuke (ふくすけ) in Okage Yokocho. The noodles were very soft, and I thought the dish was average. Thick, soft noodles are typical of Ise udon, and the other udon we ate in the area was also soft.

Toba Aquarium

Around 1 p.m. we arrived at Toba Aquarium and started with the sea lion show. At the time, the aquarium had two sea otters, Mei (メイ) and Kira (キラ).

The walruses we saw that afternoon were adorable too. They made popping sounds and even blew bubbles.

Update, November 7, 2026: A week after our visit, the aquarium announced that Kira had died on November 5, aged 18, leaving Mei as its only sea otter. According to the November 6 announcement, Mei remains off display, with no reopening date set. If you plan to see the sea otters, check the official website before you go.

Fried Shrimp at Sazanami

Mikimoto Pearl Island is also near the aquarium, but we only had time for the aquarium and dinner. Just before 5 p.m., we went to Sazanami (漣) in Toba, known for its thick, breaded fried shrimp. The shrimp were huge and delicious, and the meal was memorable. I would not drive to Toba just for this restaurant, but I recommend it if you are already visiting the aquarium.

Mt. Gozaisho

The weather had been good since October 27. On Thursday evening, we saw that rain was forecast for Friday but decided to keep our plans. The next day was cold and rainy, just as forecast.

It was about 14°C when we left the car. I was dressed the same as on the previous days and felt cold in the rain. Worried that it would be even colder higher up, I wondered whether going up the mountain was worthwhile in this weather. My friend was wearing warm clothes bought the day before; I had not bought any.

We took the Gozaisho Ropeway around 12:15 p.m. and reached Sanjo Koen Station at 12:30 p.m. On the way, the gondola passed through a low bank of what looked like cloud, and everything around us became bright white. I was not sure whether it was cloud, but it was an unusual experience. It was still raining at the top, so we had lunch first. I tried gohei mochi (五平餅) for the first time: rice pressed into a patty, skewered on bamboo, and coated in sauce. I liked that you could still feel the individual rice grains. We also ordered curry udon and pork udon, both with soft noodles. Later, I bought gohei mochi to take home as well.

After lunch, we took photos in the summit park. It was still cold, but the weather gradually improved. We could see a higher summit across a grassy ski slope with no snow, so we walked up toward it. A single-seat sightseeing chairlift was also available, but the walk was not especially tiring. It was cool and cloudy, with no strong sun. I enjoyed photographing the scenery as we climbed. We eventually reached the summit of Mt. Gozaisho, 1,212 m above sea level, and took photos of its first-order triangulation point, a surveying marker. Reaching the top gave me a small sense of achievement.

We took the sightseeing chairlift back for JPY 400 one way. It began raining again during the ride, then grew heavier, so we held umbrellas as we rode back to the ropeway station. We had still photographed the scenery we wanted and reached the summit. The visit turned out better than I had expected at the start.

The ropeway station has a display about its connection with Taiwan's Maokong Gondola. The two operators signed a friendship agreement in 2018. As a visitor from Taiwan, I enjoyed seeing the familiar name. Before heading down the mountain, I spent JPY 500 on a photo booth picture.

Nagashima Outlet

Around 3:40 p.m., we arrived at Mitsui Outlet Park Jazz Dream Nagashima and spent some time browsing and eating. We had Panda Express for dinner, which I had previously tried in San Jose. One serving cost JPY 1,255, roughly TWD 250, and was large enough for us to share.

Nabana no Sato Illuminations

It was raining that evening, but we had already bought advance tickets, so we decided to go to Nabana no Sato anyway. We left the outlet at 5:30 p.m. and arrived at 6:30 p.m. Advance tickets are JPY 2,500, including a JPY 1,000 voucher for use in the park and greenhouse admission.

Nabana no Sato is a good place to visit as a couple. LED lights fill the grounds. It reminded me of a light festival I had visited in Korea, but with real flowers incorporated into the displays. On that visit to Korea, it was -5°C and there were no flowers.

In the indoor Begonia Garden, I saw more unusual begonias than I had ever seen before. The greenhouse smelled lovely, and we took photos there. An indoor Christmas light show played three Christmas songs and created a cheerful atmosphere.

The Tunnel of Light, Hana Kairo (華回廊), is about 200 m long, with bulbs in petal-shaped sockets. It was the first tunnel of this kind I had seen, and you can take a lot of beautiful photos here.

The park was beautiful and pleasantly cool, although the rain was a drawback. I imagine it gets very crowded in good weather. There were plenty of cars and tour buses that evening, but the park itself did not feel crowded. We left at 8:10 p.m., after almost two hours.

Dormy Inn Yokkaichi

Dormy Inn offers free late-night ramen, called yonaki soba (夜鳴きそば). We had some at 9:30 p.m. It was good and saved us another trip out for food. Popsicles are available after an evening bath, while probiotic drinks such as Yakult are offered in the morning. I did not use the baths that morning.

Ganso Iganiku Kanaya

October 31 was sunny and hot again. We spent most of the morning driving, with a brief stop at Seki-juku, a former post town on the Tokaido route. We also bought food to take home at Michi-no-Eki Sekijuku (道の駅 関宿). Neither stop left much to write about.

At noon, we had sukiyaki at the main branch of Ganso Iganiku Kanaya (元祖伊賀肉 金谷) in Iga. The meal cost JPY 24,200 for two, including the service of a staff member cooking at our table. The total also included JPY 5,000 for matsutake mushrooms. Their texture was hard to describe: firm and slightly crisp, with a distinctive aroma. I enjoyed them, but I would not have thought to order them without someone recommending them. They seemed expensive.

Iga Ueno Castle

We arrived at Iga Ueno Castle at 2 p.m., and adult admission was JPY 600. By then, I was a little tired of visiting castles. After using AI to research the castle, I learned how Kawasaki Katsu had used his own money to rebuild the main tower for his hometown. I was not very interested in the history of the feudal lords, but I admired his commitment to the town. The castle also has stone walls about 30 m high and an old well in the smaller tower.

Background: Iga Ueno Castle's Two Eras

The following comes from the information boards I photographed on site and the guide booklet, checked against material from the Iga City Board of Education and the Iga Ueno Castle website.

The five-story main tower Todo Takatora was building collapsed in a storm in 1612, shortly before completion. It was not rebuilt during the Edo period. The stone walls, about 30 m high, survive today.

The current three-story main tower was built in 1935. Kawasaki Katsu, a member of the House of Representatives from the area, funded its construction with his own money and help from supporters. It is a wooden tower built in a traditional castle style, officially named Iga Bunka Sangyo Castle (伊賀文化産業城, Iga Cultural and Industrial Castle). He hoped it would promote local culture and industry.

Villagers in Koka District, Shiga Prefecture, and Ayama District, Mie Prefecture, proposed a bronze statue to thank Kawasaki for securing agricultural irrigation. Okugawa Kichisaburo, a forestry businessman from Kishu, also offered to fund renovations to his home. Kawasaki directed these funds toward rebuilding the castle, along with money from selling his calligraphy, paintings, and antiques. He kept every item connected to Matsuo Basho. Basho was born in Iga, and Kawasaki later paid for the Haiseiden, a hall honoring the poet.

As a student, Edogawa Ranpo helped with Kawasaki's newspaper, Jichi Shinbun. Kawasaki also helped him find work on several occasions. Ranpo once said that Kawasaki was the only person he considered truly worthy of being called his teacher.

According to legend, three secret tunnels connect the Shinobi Well (忍之井) in the smaller tower to places outside the castle. In 2017, the television show Tantei! Knight Scoop investigated whether a well at a nearby home connected to it. No passage was found, so the tunnels remain a legend.

Gyoza no Ohsho

We finished visiting the castle around 3 p.m., then browsed souvenirs at Iga Hyakka (伊賀百貨), inside SAKAKURA BASE in the former Ueno City Hall. At 5 p.m., we reached Frespo Sakurai shopping center, where we visited ABC-MART and the Lopia supermarket. We ate at the Nara Sakurai branch of Gyoza no Ohsho (餃子の王将) at 6 p.m., then visited MEGA Don Quijote Sakurai. I later learned that Gyoza no Ohsho and Osaka Ohsho are separate companies.

I really liked Ohsho's fried rice, which was not too oily. The gyoza were less memorable. We also ordered stir-fried noodles, but they were firm enough to seem undercooked, a little like deep-fried yi mein, a type of Chinese egg noodle.

That night we checked in at the Route Inn in Sakurai.

Day 9: Nagai Botanical Garden and the Flight Home

Sunday, November 1 was another day of good weather. In the morning, we shopped at BicCamera in Ario Yao and bought some items on behalf of others. It was the first day of Japan's new tax refund system, so we paid the tax-inclusive price at checkout. I have explained the full process in my Chinese-language guide to Japan's 2026 tax refund system. We had enjoyed Saizeriya, so we ate there again at Ario Yao for lunch. In the afternoon, we visited Nagai Botanical Garden in Osaka.

Nagai Park is free to enter, while the botanical garden inside charges JPY 300 for adults. I enjoyed the garden. We encountered quite a few visitors from Taiwan, although most of the visitors we noticed were from China.

While exploring, my friend and I discussed how Japan and mainland China turn natural scenery into paid attractions. By comparison, you can enjoy the shore of Sun Moon Lake in Taiwan without buying a ticket. We wondered whether people in Taiwan would still visit if similar places charged admission.

That evening, we returned the car at Kansai Airport. The new tax refund process was straightforward. Before checking our bags, we used the tax-free kiosk at the airport. The screen showed a green result, meaning no inspection was required. After that, we just had to wait for the refund to reach the credit card. By 7:44 p.m., we were waiting at the airport.

Dessert with the meal on our return flight, CI177, was Marufuku pudding from Osaka's Marufuku Coffee (丸福珈琲店). We had not received it on the outbound flight, CI176. We landed in Kaohsiung at 10:40 p.m.

How the Costs Compare

I think our spending was reasonable, though slightly above average, mainly because of the cost of driving. The Japan Tourism Agency's survey for July to September 2026 excludes international airfare and reports average spending of JPY 30,785 per person per night for leisure visitors from Taiwan. Excluding airfare, we spent about JPY 265,800 each, or JPY 33,200 per person per night across eight nights, about 8% more. Our transport costs alone averaged about JPY 7,300 per person per night, compared with the survey average of JPY 2,900. That difference of JPY 4,400 was larger than the gap in total spending.

Accommodation was not unusually expensive. We spent about JPY 8,900 per person per night, below the survey average of JPY 9,898. The six nights with breakfast but no dinner averaged about JPY 11,472 per room. That was also below the JPY 14,803 average for business hotels reported by Tokyo Shoko Research for April to June 2026. Meals out averaged just TWD 691 per person per day because all seven hotels included breakfast and two included dinner. The meal at Kanaya accounted for about 40% of our dining expenses.

What's Worth Visiting

A road trip gives you the flexibility to explore as long as someone can drive. I enjoyed Wakayama, which was not crowded. Places became busier as we got closer to Osaka.

Nachi Falls was spectacular, and both the falls and Kumano Nachi Taisha are essential stops. The climb was hot and sunny but worthwhile. Onigajo was excellent too, although the walk was tiring.

At Wakayama Castle, I climbed stone steps in the rain, then struggled to understand the Japanese history displays. The visit left little impression. The story of Kawasaki Katsu rebuilding Iga Ueno Castle interested me more. Wakayama Castle Park Zoo is free and worth visiting, while Ouchiyama Zoo does not justify a special trip. Most of the rock formations only need a short photo stop, so I would not allow too much time for them. Meoto Iwa was the more interesting of those stops.

Sazanami's fried shrimp was excellent. Saizeriya offered good food at low prices, and I also liked Ohsho's fried rice. I strongly advise against the ama hut.
