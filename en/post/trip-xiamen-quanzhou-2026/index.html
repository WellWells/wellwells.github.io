Chinese New Year Mini Three Links: A 5-Day Trip to Xiamen & Quanzhou

Took the Mini Three Links over LNY—5 days in Xiamen & Quanzhou, 3 people under USD 1,620. Kinmen's new pier guide, Gulangyu chaos, and one wildlife zoo worth every minute.

This Chinese New Year (Day 2 through Day 6 of the holiday), the three of us—two adults and one elderly family member—decided to take the Mini Three Links route to Xiamen and Quanzhou. We chose this route mainly because flight tickets are generally expensive during the New Year period, and the cheaper international flights usually require taking the high-speed rail up to Taoyuan Airport first, which is a hassle.

The Mini Three Links flies from Kaohsiung to Kinmen on a domestic route and then takes a ferry to Xiamen. The round-trip cost per person was about USD 191.54 / CNY 1,276.95, very reasonable even at Lunar New Year prices. We figured we'd try a different route, and off we went.

Mini Three Links Transportation

The Mini Three Links is quite different from a regular international flight. It involves two legs: a domestic flight to Kinmen, then a ferry to Xiamen. More involved, but the price makes it worthwhile.

Brand New in 2026: Kinmen Port Passenger Terminal

Important information: The new Kinmen Port Passenger Terminal has been in trial operation since February 3, 2026. The old "Shuitou Pier" terminal that everyone was familiar with no longer handles departures or arrivals!

The new terminal is 3.5 times larger than the old pier (built at a cost of ~USD 90.7 million), with the immigration flow rebuilt to airport standards. Key changes to be aware of:

  1. Arrive at least 1.5 hours early: The new terminal is spacious with longer check-in and immigration lines, so give yourself plenty of buffer time.
  2. Multi-level flow separation (no more mixing):
    • [Departure (to Xiamen)] Head to the 3rd floor: Taxis can follow the signage and drive up the ramp directly to the 3rd-floor departure hall.
    • [Arrival (entering Kinmen)] Arrive on the 1st floor: Arriving passengers should follow the floor signage to avoid getting lost. Staff are on hand to help guide you through.
  3. Fast clearance, great facilities: During peak hours, the terminal can handle 2,800 people per hour in both directions, with new e-Gates for automated immigration. There's also a 7-Eleven, duty-free shops (Ever Rich), restaurants, a family waiting area, and charging stations. It's a clear step up from the old terminal.
  4. Parking info: Parking is free for all of February, with smart billing starting in March (supporting license plate recognition, EasyCard, LINE Pay, etc.).

Transport Cost Comparison with Direct Flights

Our total transport cost for three people was USD 484.50 / CNY 3,229.98. Compared to direct flights from Kaohsiung during Lunar New Year—which can easily cost over USD 324 / CNY 2,160 per person—the Mini Three Links may be more complicated, but the price advantage is clear.

Assuming you depart from Kaohsiung around 7 AM in both scenarios, here's a quick comparison:

In comparison, the Mini Three Links round-trip costs less than half of a direct flight. For travelers with flexible schedules, trading an extra 1.5 hours each way for savings of nearly USD 229.05 per person is an attractive deal (especially during LNY when prices spike).

Map & Itinerary

The trip was split into two halves: first two nights in Quanzhou, last two nights in Xiamen. With an elderly family member in tow, we kept the pace relaxed, though the LNY crowds still took a toll: queuing was the norm, and ride-hailing was worse.

We left the house at 5:20 AM. The drive from downtown Kaohsiung to Siaogang Airport takes about an hour, but at that hour there's barely any traffic. We took the domestic flight to Kinmen, grabbed a taxi to the Kinmen Port Passenger Terminal (Shuitou Pier), and the whole process went smoothly. We waited at the terminal until 10:30 to board, and the ferry was full.

After arriving at Xiamen's Wutong Pier, we hailed a car to Xiamen Railway Station and took the high-speed rail north to Quanzhou.

On the ride to the hotel in Quanzhou, we spotted rows of yellow bikes with the "YouBike" logo. Turns out Quanzhou's bike-sharing system "Little Yellow Man" is an export of Taiwan's YouBike 1.0 hardware, with fully localized operations: pay via Quantong Card or WeChat, no deposit, first hour free. Seeing familiar Taiwanese bikes across the strait was a fun surprise.

After checking in, we took a short nap. By evening it was getting colder and most attractions close at 5 PM, so we opted for a stroll around the mall. For dinner, we chose Mumachuan Rotating Hot Pot & BBQ: a conveyor belt hot pot plus BBQ joint with 130+ ingredients all-you-can-eat. At this price point, you'd be lucky to get a basic set meal in Taiwan; here, it's an all-you-can-eat hot pot and grill. Great value. After dinner, we swung by UNIQLO at Wanda Plaza for some heat-tech innerwear. Quanzhou in February was colder than expected. Grabbed a Guming bubble tea on the way back, and that wrapped up Day 1.

This was the most packed day of the trip. In the morning, we headed to the Maritime Silk Road Wildlife World in Quanzhou.

The biggest highlight of this zoo is the interactive experiences. Every zone offers hands-on animal encounters. The most memorable for me was touching kangaroos up close. They were docile and completely unfazed. The meerkat enclosure had an open design without excessive glass barriers, so you could watch them from close range. There were also opportunities to interact with ring-tailed lemurs; the interaction design here is well thought out. The alpaca and predator feeding zones cost extra, so we skipped those.

For lunch, we ate at the Red Panda Restaurant inside the park. I ordered a bowl of beef noodles and it was an absolute letdown: when I bit into the "beef," the texture was completely fake, covered in a thick batter. That bowl was worth maybe USD 1.94 at best. Amusement park food quality, I suppose.

After leaving the zoo, we headed to Kaiyuan Temple. During Chinese New Year, it was absolutely mobbed. The temple itself is worth seeing, and the Zhenguo Pagoda is the most striking landmark. The plaques and stone carvings inside preserve quite a few traditional Chinese characters, original artifacts from the Qing Dynasty and Republican era. The area around the temple was lively, especially West Street. The entire street is lined with shops, food stalls, and people. We tried some osmanthus cake on West Street, available in osmanthus flower, longan, and sesame flavors. It tasted basically the same as Taiwan's scholar cakes: steamed glutinous rice in a different shape.

Next, we took a car to Luoyang Bridge, arriving around 5:30 PM with the sun already setting. This bridge's defining feature is its sheer length: approximately 731 meters. We wandered around the nearby old streets for a bit, and by the time we'd walked the entire length of the bridge, it was already 6:30 PM.

Dinner that evening was a headache. Back in the old town, everywhere had a queue. We originally wanted ginger duck near the Guan Gong Temple, but the area was packed and there was no way we'd get a table. Even worse, most ride-hail drivers refused to enter the old town: getting a car was difficult. After a long wait on the roadside, we finally hailed a ride and decided to just eat Haidilao near our hotel.

The Haidilao experience was consistent as always. After a full day of running around, sitting down for a proper meal was satisfaction enough.

Heads up: During Chinese New Year, unless you have a specific reason, avoid the old town area. Crowds, endless queues, and impossible ride-hailing make for a poor experience.

We started the morning at ICBC. A family member's debit card from when they worked in China had expired and needed in-person renewal. Quick note: almost all banking in China requires a local mobile number, so sort this out before your trip if needed.

After finishing at the bank, we took a car to the Quanzhou Intangible Cultural Heritage Museum, which is free to enter. The exhibitions focus on traditional Minnan culture—paper art, lanterns, hand puppet theater—things that Taiwan actually shares with this region, giving me an oddly nostalgic feeling. The one that surprised me was the paper weaving art, a craft I'd never encountered before. Puppet theater is also rarely seen in Taiwan, and there were some finely made bamboo vases. The exhibits were interesting, though the holiday crowds detracted from the experience.

At noon, we finally got to try the Sidan Ginger Duck we'd missed the day before. We waited about 30 minutes, but once seated, food came quickly. The flavor leans heavily charred, bold, and oily. If you're traveling with elderly family, be warned. Personally, I quite enjoyed it, though I'd give it an overall 80 out of 100. Worth trying once, but not worth a special trip.

After lunch, we headed to Quanzhou's high-speed rail station and killed time at a Starbucks while waiting for our train. A family member collects Starbucks cards, so they picked up a Quanzhou-edition gift card.

We took the high-speed rail south to Xiamen, checked into the Marco Polo Xiamen, and dropped off our luggage. Then we headed out to Jimei School Village. To be honest, this spot is basically just an Instagram photo op. If you can skip it, skip it. We did stumble upon a quite good starfruit jelly drink here, so not a total loss.

For dinner, we went to Zuideyi Camellia Oil Chicken, which turned out to be the surprise hit restaurant of the entire trip. Cheap, full-flavored, and it goes with rice. The three of us ate until we were stuffed. Highly recommended. Afterward, we stopped by the nearby Yonghui Supermarket to stock up on snacks and grabbed some freshly baked bread for a late-night bite.

In the morning, we took a car to Dongdu Ferry Terminal. Here's the practical info on getting to Gulangyu:

Gulangyu Ticketing Guide

🎟️ Key Ticketing Info (strongly recommend booking in advance):

  • We bought our February 21 tickets on February 7. During peak seasons like Chinese New Year, it gets extremely crowded: book early to avoid being shut out at the pier!
  • Official channel: Search for "嶼見廈門" mini-program on WeChat or Alipay (mainland-only).
  • Advance purchase window: Tickets go on sale 15 days in advance (e.g., Feb 1 for Feb 15 tickets).
  • Pricing & limits: Tourist tickets are USD 5.25 or USD 12.00 (both include return trip). One account can hold a maximum of 10 unused tickets.

🚢 Pier Selection Guide:

  • Daytime boarding (07:10–17:30):
    • Dongdu Ferry Terminal: The most commonly used pier for tourists, with frequent departures going directly to "Sanqiutian" or "Neicuo'ao" piers on the island.
    • Pier No. 1: Close to Zhongshan Road Pedestrian Street, primarily serves the "Neicuo'ao" pier.
    • Songyu Ferry Terminal: Located in the Haicang district, convenient for those already staying in Haicang.
  • Nighttime boarding (after 17:50): Daytime terminals close in the evening. The only option for boarding at night is the "Lundu Port (Hall 2)."
  • On-island pier differences:
    • Sanqiutian Pier: Close to popular attractions (like "The Most Beautiful Corner" and the Longtou Road shopping area), the top choice for most visitors. It has 24-hour ferry service back to the mainland (nighttime ferries go to Lundu Port).
    • Neicuo'ao Pier: Near beaches and natural scenery, ideal for those who prefer a quieter stroll or are staying at guesthouses on the west side of the island.

⚡ Important Reminders:

  • Timing trap: Boarding closes 5 minutes before departure! Chinese New Year queues are extremely long, so arrive at the pier 45–60 minutes early for security screening. Don't cut it close.
  • Your ID is your ticket: After purchasing tickets with your ID (e.g., Taiwan Compatriot Permit / Residence Permit), simply scan your ID at the gate, no physical ticket needed. If you forget your ID, you can use an Alipay boarding code or get a temporary pass.
  • Don't accidentally buy resident tickets: Only holders of a Xiamen ID, social security card, or residence permit can purchase the CNY 8 resident ticket. Regular tourists should never select the resident ticket option, or you'll be unable to board at the pier.

We didn't do much research before going to the island and just bought tickets for Sunlight Rock and Shuzhuang Garden. The result: both were pretty disappointing. The main reason was that Chinese New Year meant way too many people.

To catch the view at Sunlight Rock, we stood in line for a full 90 minutes before reaching the top. Along the way, we witnessed quite a few "spectacles": people blatantly smoking in the queue despite the rules, cutting in line, and even arguing with each other. But since we were trapped in the queue with nothing to do, we took the opportunity to order souvenirs on our phones and have them delivered to the hotel. Once we finally reached the top, the view wasn't anything particularly special.

As for Shuzhuang Garden, I'd assumed from the name that it would be full of flowers, but it's actually mostly historical exhibits and traditional Chinese garden landscaping. The LNY crowds made it impossible to appreciate anything. We just shuffled along with everyone else.

Lunch on the island was a Gulangyu specialty meal. We waited forever and the taste was just average.

In the afternoon, we took the ferry back to Lundu Port and walked to Zhongshan Road Pedestrian Street. Worth a stroll for the festive atmosphere, but the shops mainly cater to tourists. We had Minnan snacks on Zhongshan Road—Quanzhou fish balls, five-spice rolls, satay noodles, oyster omelette, and rice dumplings—about what you'd expect from a LNY tourist zone. The five-spice rolls in particular were so bad we gave up and left them unfinished.

Back at the hotel, still feeling hungry, we ordered McDonald's delivery via Meituan, and that's how the Gulangyu day ended.

On our last day, we took a car to the Xiamen Botanical Garden.

This was my favorite attraction in Xiamen. Still crowded, but leagues better than Gulangyu. The park has a lush tropical rainforest area and a unique desert plant area with diverse, well-maintained plant species. We entered through the west gate and exited via the south gate. Due to crowd control, the park was basically one-way traffic, a well-planned layout that kept the walk smooth. It was still Lunar New Year, so the crowds were unavoidable. Great place for tropical rainforest photos.

For lunch, we took a car to MixC Mall and ended up at Hudaodao Butter Clay Pot Seafood. One dish left a lasting impression: wasabi pork collar. It had that distinctive wasabi fragrance without being uncomfortably spicy, and it went well with rice. The only minor complaint was that it was a tad oily, but it was still good.

After lunch, we headed back to the hotel to grab our luggage, then took a car to Wutong Pier. We caught the 5 PM ferry back to Kinmen, connected to our flight back to Kaohsiung, and arrived home around 9:30 PM. Five days, done.

A few numbers stood out:

  • Mini Three Links round-trip transport (flights + ferry): About USD 191.54 per adult (seniors get 50% off), the best value-for-money part of the entire trip.
  • Food & Dining: USD 309.61 total for three people over five days, or about USD 20.64 per person per day. Individual meals ranged from roughly USD 3.66–15.26 per person, with most falling between USD 6.48–10.69.
  • Ride-hailing: About 25 rides total, USD 101.34 combined, averaging USD 4.05 per ride. Convenient and cheap, except in the old town during LNY, where it was an exercise in futility.
  • Accommodation: Two nights at Home Inn Plus Quanzhou for USD 230.10, two nights at Marco Polo Xiamen for USD 286.84. Split three ways, that's less than USD 64.80 per person per night.

💡 Practical tips for booking hotels and tickets: If your itinerary is confirmed, book early. Beyond the usual international platforms (Agoda, Booking, Trip.com), I'd recommend checking these two major Chinese platforms, which often have exclusive deals or combo tickets:

  1. Ctrip: China's largest online travel platform, offering hotels, flights, and attraction tickets with the widest coverage. Supports international credit card payments.
  2. Fliggy: Alibaba Group's travel platform: great for booking flights, train tickets, and attraction passes. All transactions are secured through Alipay escrow.

⚠️ Important note: Due to Chinese regulations, not all hotels are licensed to accept guests from Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan, or foreign countries. When searching for accommodation on these platforms, make sure to filter for "Applicable to: Taiwanese guests" to avoid showing up only to be turned away at check-in.

Conclusion

The one word that sums up traveling in China during Chinese New Year: crowds. Gulangyu was so packed you couldn't enjoy anything, the old town queues made us give up entirely, and hailing a ride took at least 30 minutes. If you're planning to visit popular attractions during Chinese New Year, steel yourself mentally in advance.

Setting the crowds aside, this trip still had plenty of memorable moments. The interactive design at the Maritime Silk Road Wildlife World was a pleasant surprise: getting to touch kangaroos and meerkats up close is something you'd rarely experience in Taiwan, and that alone made it worth the ticket. The Quanzhou Intangible Cultural Heritage Museum also deserves a mention; exhibits like paper weaving art, puppet theater, and lanterns gave us a deeper appreciation for traditional Minnan culture. Xiamen Botanical Garden, with its tropical rainforest and desert plant areas, was my top pick among Xiamen attractions. Food-wise, Mumachuan and Zuideyi were both good value for the money, and the wasabi pork collar unexpectedly became the culinary highlight of the trip.

Jimei School Village is just a social media photo spot and not worth a dedicated visit. Gulangyu during peak season is a miserable experience. And the old town during Chinese New Year? Just don't.

There were a few observations from this trip that stood out, things I'd noticed before but felt even more acutely this time:

  • Cigarette smoke everywhere: This was the biggest culture shock. In Taiwan, you basically never smell smoke in public places, but in China, even in areas marked non-smoking, the smell is omnipresent. At Sunlight Rock, someone lit up while queuing and got called out by frustrated visitors. Hotel-wise, Home Inn Plus in Quanzhou had lingering smoke smell while the Marco Polo Xiamen was fine. If you're sensitive to smoke, choose your hotel carefully.
  • Rushing onto the train before people get off: When the high-speed rail doors opened, passengers outside immediately started pushing in without waiting for anyone to exit. No concept of "let people off first" whatsoever.
  • Littering without a second thought: I witnessed a child rip open a wrapper and toss the packaging straight onto the ground, with the adult next to them not batting an eye. That's something you'd rarely see in Taiwan.
  • Vandalism at scenic areas: Even at the well-maintained botanical garden, we repeatedly saw visitors breaking off plant parts to "test" whether they were real or fake. This kind of thoughtless behavior really dampens the mood.
  • The Gulangyu public restroom horror: In a public restroom on Gulangyu, there was feces on top of the urinal. I have no words.

The Mini Three Links as a travel route was an interesting choice. It's cheap and spares you the trip to Taoyuan Airport, but more complicated, so it's best suited for people who aren't pressed for time.

Traveling with an elderly family member meant we deliberately slowed the pace. Some stops would've been squeezed tighter with just younger travelers.
