Preface

The reason for this trip was simple: my company had one spot available for NVIDIA GTC 2026 in San Jose, and I took it. It was my first time visiting the United States. What worried me most before leaving wasn't the itinerary, it was what my family would think, and they turned out to be completely supportive.

GTC essentially took over downtown San Jose: roads were closed, SAP Center was packed with tens of thousands of people, all there for Jensen Huang's two-hour keynote. At the venue, you could turn around at any moment and find yourself next to the CEO of a startup or a leading expert, everyone talking about technologies that are reshaping entire industries. I went feeling excited, but came back with a much clearer sense of what I want to do next.

This post documents what I saw and experienced at GTC, captures impressions from the trip, and reflects on some career-related thoughts.

Itinerary Overview

The trip spanned eight days and seven nights, roughly divided into three phases: San Francisco sightseeing, GTC conference, and Silicon Valley exploration.

Day 1: Taoyuan → San Francisco

Departure

Early morning at Taoyuan Terminal 2. I boarded an EVA Air Boeing 777 on a direct flight to San Francisco. I browsed the duty-free shops after security, then parked myself at the gate. The 11-hour flight was more manageable than expected. I slept most of it, and when I was awake, I watched Welcome to Derry, which had a good pace. EVA's in-flight meal was up to its usual standard. Somehow it didn't feel that long before we landed.

First Impressions of San Francisco

Landing at SFO—my first step on American soil. The feeling was oddly surreal. Customs went smoother than I expected; the questions were basic: how long are you staying, when are you leaving, what brings you here. Simple English got me through. Baggage claim was another story. I waited nearly an hour. Easily the most drawn-out part of the whole arrival.

After the airport, we drove to the hotel to check in and drop off luggage, then headed into the city. First stop: the Golden Gate Bridge.

Golden Gate Bridge

Since this was a business trip, there was no time to linger like a proper tourist. I took a few photos, felt the scale in person, then grabbed a latte at a nearby café and moved on (I spent longer ordering the coffee than looking at the bridge).

Seeing it in person is genuinely different from photos. The red steel structure appearing through the Bay Area fog had a presence that no screen can quite replicate.

Palace of Fine Arts

Next up: the Palace of Fine Arts. Originally built for the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition, it was later reconstructed and preserved. A beautiful building with great afternoon light, a lake and a lawn around it. It reads more like a park than a monument.

Union Square and the City

Next was Union Square in downtown San Francisco. The main goal was the Nintendo Store: walked through, noted that prices are basically higher than Japan, bought nothing.

Walking around, the first thing that made me stop and take a photo wasn't the square itself, but a massive ChatGPT billboard hanging at the most visible spot in the city center. Hard to miss.

Lunch was near Fisherman's Wharf: Clam Chowder in a Bread Bowl, the classic San Francisco dish. Thick clam chowder served inside a hollowed-out sourdough loaf. The bread soaks up the soup, you eat the whole thing together. Salty, comforting, slightly tangy, and the soup-soaked bread is the best part.

After that we wandered around Pier 39. Sea lions were piled up on the wooden docks, completely unbothered by the crowd of tourists watching them.

Checking into San Jose

That evening, we drove back to San Jose and returned to Hampton Inn San Jose Cherry Ave, where we'd already checked in that morning. The hotel sits right off Highway 85—heavy traffic in all directions. Getting food meant crossing a massive eight-lane road. The drive to the GTC venue was about 15 minutes, so location was fine overall. Standard American business hotel. Dinner was Panda Express nearby.

My first Panda Express in America. Decent, heavy-flavored, nothing remarkable—it did the job. What caught my eye was the hiring sign on the door, listing salary ranges that reflect the local cost of living:

Day 2: GTC Check-in and Welcome Reception

Breakfast and the Neighborhood

Hampton Inn includes free breakfast: scrambled eggs, bacon, waffles, juice. There's a self-serve waffle maker: pour the batter in, flip it, done. Simple, filling.

Lunch was a bowl of Vietnamese beef pho at a nearby Pho Kitchen, around TWD 544. Honestly one of the more disappointing meals on the trip. The broth was mediocre, the beef unremarkable. Compared to a random pho shop in Taiwan, this one didn't come close. Very poor value.

On the way out, I noticed a sign in the parking lot warning against smash-and-grab theft, a reminder to keep valuables out of your car.

GTC Check-in

In the afternoon, we headed to the San Jose Convention Center to check in. You could spot the GTC event banners hanging from the street lights well before reaching the venue. Inside, I queued at the General Attendees counter, picked up my badge, and got a few drink vouchers with it.

Welcome Reception

The GTC welcome reception was, more than anything, a large-scale networking event. The venue served a signature NVIDIA cocktail, the Nvidiarita: green, tasting like a margarita base with something undefined mixed in.

Most people drifted around with drinks, exchanging cards, delivering micro-pitches to potential investors: getting your value proposition across in a few sentences. We naturally ended up talking mostly with people from Asia; the shared background made conversation easier.

Day 3: GTC Keynote, Jensen's Stage

The Queue

We left early, because Jensen's keynote is the centerpiece of GTC. When we arrived, there was already a long line stretching from the convention center out into the surrounding blocks. Once inside, the sheer scale of an arena that holds tens of thousands of people hit me. Seats were nearly full. The air conditioning was blasting, and after walking around outside all morning, sitting down in there was a relief.

The person seated next to me turned out to be an Asian American who had moved from Beijing at age three, now working on robotics at a startup. One random conversation in a GTC seat, and you're already hearing an interesting story.

Jensen Takes the Stage

Being there in person is a completely different experience. The stage presence is undeniable. His pacing, timing of pauses, sense of humor, all calibrated with precision. Yet it never feels rehearsed. There's an engineer's directness underneath everything, a genuine enthusiasm that comes through.

The two-hour keynote didn't have a single dull moment. Jensen went through the growth of the inference market, the hardware spec jumps, the "AI factory" concept that ties compute and storage together, and the Google Cloud partnership at the infrastructure layer.

The takeaway was that NVIDIA is done being only a chip company. Hardware and software ecosystem are being built out together, so that NVIDIA ends up as the infrastructure of the AI era.

Near the end, Jensen started introducing a mascot character—that's when I slipped out early to go take an exam. GTC offers free certification exams during the conference, so I took the NVIDIA NCA-GENL. I wrote a separate post with everything you need to know: NVIDIA NCA-GENL Certification: 20-Minute Passing Guide and Essential Notes.

Humanoid Robot Forum

After the keynote, the afternoon session was a humanoid robot expert panel at San Jose Civic, a venue with a great retro theater vibe. Outside, an OpenMind humanoid robot was on display, just sitting there at rest.

Dinner: DoughZone

On the way back, I spotted a few cats hanging around by a wooden building—anti-cat spikes were laid out nearby, but the cats had settled in right beside them and didn't care at all.

Dinner was at DoughZone (豆乳坊), a Chinese noodle chain. The soy beef wrap was solid, just slightly awkward eating alone.

Day 4: GTC Exhibition

The main event today was the exhibition hall. GTC's exhibition was much larger than I'd imagined, with booths from chip giants to early-stage startups.

Exhibition Highlights

ASUS was showcasing AI POD server racks. ASUS is going deeper and deeper into AI infrastructure. Foxconn's booth was impressive too, covering not just servers and infrastructure but also humanoid robot demos.

I stopped by the Microsoft booth and grabbed some Azure-branded snacks. It was much needed. I'd been on my feet all day, and besides the free coffee, the exhibition hall's free Snack Bar (mostly Microsoft-stocked) saved me from hunger more than once.

Session Hopping

I spent the day moving between different conference rooms for breakout sessions. The overall feeling was a bit like attending university lectures, sitting in the audience while a presenter walked through their company's technology or research direction.

Some sessions were genuinely valuable, giving you a new perspective to take home. Others didn't land as well—topics too far removed from my current work. One session on the history of CUDA was mostly background and context with little technical substance; I was fighting yawns by the end and left the moment it finished.

Dinner

That evening I tried The Kebab Shop and ordered a grilled chicken salad bowl. After several consecutive days of enormous portions, I deliberately went lighter. It was good.

Day 5: AI Forum

Face to Face with AI Leaders

This was the part of GTC I found most worthwhile.

Visible in the photo: Harrison Chase (LangChain CEO), Mira Murati (former OpenAI CTO), and Aravind Srinivas (Perplexity CEO).

Listening to them discuss their views on the direction of AI, you could feel very directly that these are people who build the thing themselves. The depth of the discussion made me realize how much catching up I still have to do in terms of technical perspective.

Dinner

That night I ordered a bacon cheeseburger with cheese fries—the add-on portion was slightly more than I could finish. On the walk back to the hotel I noticed jacaranda trees in full bloom. California's March weather really is something.

Day 6: Stanford University & Computer History Museum

Day 6 was the final day of GTC. The exhibition halls and breakout sessions were noticeably smaller in scale, fewer people, and the whole venue had a more open feel. I caught a few morning sessions and called it early.

In the morning, we headed to Stanford University. The campus is enormous. You can just keep walking. We mainly checked out Hoover Tower and the Memorial Court.

Honestly, no overwhelming feeling, just a vast university campus with comfortable weather and good walking. Lunch was Subway, eaten sitting somewhere on campus.

In the afternoon, we drove to Mountain View for the Computer History Museum.

A few exhibits I stood in front of for a long time:

Enigma Cipher Machine (1935)

Enigma was the electromechanical encryption device Germany used heavily through WWII, built from a keyboard, rotors, a plugboard, and a lamp board. The one on display is a 1935 model, close enough to see how the rotors and plugboard were put together.

Bosch ABS Electronic Controller (1978)

Anyone who's worked on automotive electronics will feel something looking at this board. A 1978 ABS control unit PCB—incomparably simpler than today's automotive ECUs, but the foundational concept was laid right here.

The Evolution of Storage

From massive disk platters to IBM RAMAC drives, then optical and floppy disks. Decades of storage evolution compressed onto a single display wall: from kilobytes to terabytes.

Core Memory

A Sperry Rand core memory board and a Whirlwind core plane—up close, those hand-threaded magnetic rings are almost unbelievable. Each tiny ring: one bit. By today's standards it's incomprehensible, but this is where memory started.

A Taiwanese Motherboard

Mid-way through the exhibit, I came across a JoinData 486DX motherboard, labeled Made in Taiwan. Seeing something from home displayed as a historical artifact in a foreign museum was a strange feeling, and it made the "world's contract manufacturer" label feel real. Taiwan's place in the technology chain started right about then.

First-Generation iPhone Teardown

A disassembled display of the original 2007 iPhone components, along with a development board. The moment Jobs unveiled the iPhone changed an entire industry, and these parts are where that change began physically.

Intel Itanium Montecito Wafer

A complete Itanium wafer, packed densely with dies. Itanium ultimately lost to x86-64, but it represents a bold architectural bet.

This museum is the highest-density engineering experience of the entire trip. If you work in software or hardware, or have any interest in the history of computing, set aside half a day for it. If the person you're traveling with isn't an engineer, though, it probably won't land and they'll be bored.

Dinner: La Victoria

That night we went to the famous La Victoria Taqueria in San Jose. I ordered the Super Burrito with their iconic orange sauce. The burrito was massive; the orange sauce had a slightly spicy, sweet-tangy kick that worked really well. I still couldn't finish it.

Day 7: Free Day

No agenda today. Just wandering through a nearby mall. At some point I walked past a Milksha (迷客夏), a Taiwanese bubble tea chain. Seeing a familiar brand that far from home was a small, genuine surprise. I walked several laps, sat down somewhere when my feet got tired, ordered a drink, and rested.

Later I swung by Trader Joe's to pick up some souvenirs, then ended the night at Jollibee, the Filipino fast food chain that hasn't made it to Taiwan yet. Their signature Chickenjoy fried chicken had crispy skin and juicy meat, a totally different style from KFC, and the portion was more than enough.

Day 8: Heading Home

California Sunrise

I woke up early on purpose, went out near the hotel, and shot a set of California sunrise photos. The fiery sunrise, the color gradient, the mountain silhouette. The sky cooperated on the last morning.

The weather throughout GTC was all sunshine: around 13°C in the morning, climbing to 30°C by noon, zero clouds. The week after we got back, temperatures started to drop again.

Return Flight

We arrived at SFO in the evening and boarded EVA Air back to Taiwan.

Reflections

On GTC

GTC runs closer to an annual home-turf show than a technical conference. From Jensen's uninterrupted two-hour keynote, to the arms race on the exhibition floor, to what gets discussed in the breakout rooms, you get a very clear sense that AI is already happening rather than coming.

The fact that downtown San Jose closed streets for GTC says everything about the scale and reach. Tens of thousands at the SAP Center keynote, CEOs and experts from around the world, startups lining up to get their demos seen: the energy and scale of this whole ecosystem is genuinely hard to imagine from Taiwan.

On the Gap in Technical Perspective

You'll encounter CEOs and technical experts across every field at the venue. These are the people actually steering industries—not short on resources, not lacking direction. Their conversations are about converting the next order-of-magnitude of compute into real solutions, and making AI actually work in concrete real-world scenarios.

What stuck with me most was their attitude toward technical challenges. Whether it's the sustained long-horizon training that AI research demands, or how Agentic AI can autonomously advance and correct itself across multi-step tasks, what they talk about is how to work through it in engineering terms, one step at a time.

What's harder is that hardware is scaling at breakneck speed, but back in actual work environments, market inertia tends to favor conservative, low-cost approaches. Excess compute becomes the reality. The technologies that look electrifying on demo stages often have a much longer road than expected to integrate into existing workflows. That gap is a reality I brought home that still needs facing.

On Career Direction

During the trip, a lot of conversations with friends who came along stretched from GTC observations into reflections on where we each are in our work lives. Everyone was at a different position and the views diverged a lot, and much of it only settled for me after I got back.

Some things come down to decisions made above, and you end up starting before the direction is certain. Rather than re-evaluating in place, better to start moving.

Coming back from GTC, I feel more grounded than I did when I left. Direction is clearer. There have always been bottlenecks in my existing technical domain that conventional methods can't break through. But seeing at GTC how AI is being applied in signal processing and decision-layer contexts made me realize this isn't about following a trend or picking an optional add-on. It's the right entry point for solving the hard problems. Worth serious investment.
