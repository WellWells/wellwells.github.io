7 Days in Chengdu and Chongqing: Jiuzhaigou, Panda Valley, and Dujiangyan

High-speed rail from Chengdu East to Huanglongjiuzhai with two seniors, a day each in Jiuzhaigou's Rize and Zechawa valleys, a private car to Panda Valley, a guide at Dujiangyan, and fritillary powder bought at a Chongqing day tour's shopping stop, later returned.

Around the 2026 Mid-Autumn Festival holiday, my family of five, including two seniors, spent seven days on a self-guided trip to Chengdu and Chongqing. We left September 23 and returned to Taiwan September 29. We flew Cathay Pacific on open-jaw tickets: into Chengdu and out of Chongqing, with a connection in Hong Kong each way.

Our outbound connection in Hong Kong was only 55 minutes. Before the trip I had checked that the minimum connecting time between two Cathay flights is 50 minutes. All five tickets were also on the same booking, with our bags checked through to Chengdu, so we kept the 55-minute connection. If we had missed the connection, though, the next flight to Chengdu might not have left until the following day, and we would have lost our high-speed rail tickets and the first night's room in Jiuzhaigou. As it turned out, CX449 arrived in Hong Kong early. We walked briskly from the plane all the way to the gate and still waited about half an hour to board.

CX986 was originally scheduled to land at Chengdu Tianfu International Airport. On July 31, Cathay moved its Chengdu flights to Chengdu Shuangliu International Airport and automatically updated the arrival airport on our booking.

I thought the chicken lunch on the Hong Kong–Chengdu leg was pretty good, and I had a can of Tsingtao with it. After landing at Shuangliu, the plane taxied for a good while before it came to a stop. We went through immigration one at a time. Officers checked each person's Mainland Travel Permit for Taiwan Residents (the "Taiwan compatriot permit") and ran a facial-recognition scan, then let us through without any trouble. The two seniors were slower getting through, so I went ahead to baggage claim. Our bags came out soon after.

I had booked an airport pickup on Fliggy before the trip. The booking was tied to our flight number and included up to 60 minutes of free waiting after landing, so a slow immigration line was nothing to worry about. We reached Chengdu East Station almost two hours before our train. While we waited, we bought bread and Chagee drinks at the mall inside the station, none of it cheap.

We bought our five high-speed rail tickets on 12306, China Railway's ticketing system, using our Taiwan compatriot permits. From Huanglongjiuzhai Station, a private car and driver we had booked on Ctrip took us to the hotel. We checked in within a few minutes of the schedule I had drawn up before the trip.

After dropping off our bags, we had some afternoon-tea snacks at the hotel. A little after 8 p.m. we went out for a walk, and at 9 we stepped into Hao Bashi Chongqing Xiaomian, a Chongqing-style noodle shop. Bashi is Sichuanese for "authentic" or "just right." I ordered a bowl of braised yak noodle soup. In mainland China the word for yak is written 牦牛 and pronounced máo niú. Taiwan writes it 犛牛, and the Ministry of Education's Revised Mandarin Chinese Dictionary gives the reading as ㄌㄧˊ ㄋㄧㄡˊ (lí niú).

Jiuzhaigou Tickets, Rides, and September Weather

Jiuzhaigou (Jiuzhai Valley National Park) tickets must be reserved in advance under each visitor's name and ID, and there is no entry without a reservation. Book and pay through the park's WeChat Official Account. Peak-season admission is CNY 190, and the park shuttle bus costs another CNY 90.

The park entrance is about a 15-minute walk from the hotel, but we took DiDi both days, at about CNY 6 a ride. A standard DiDi Express car is a five-seat sedan that takes only four passengers, so a fifth person puts it over capacity. On the first morning all five of us squeezed into one car, and the driver didn't charge extra. After that, some drivers asked for an extra fee of CNY 5 to CNY 10 for the fifth passenger. Two cars cost only CNY 12 in total. Any fee above CNY 6 makes one crowded car cost more than two, and a CNY 10 fee makes it CNY 16. With five people, just call two cars instead of paying a driver extra to squeeze into one. The only catch is that the second car has to be booked from another phone.

It was around 10°C (50°F) both days at Jiuzhaigou, and a fall jacket was enough. I'd also bring some ginger candy.

Shuzheng Valley and Rize Valley

Jiuzhaigou's three valleys form a Y. Shuzheng Valley runs up from the park entrance to Nuorilang, where Rize Valley branches off to the right and Zechawa Valley to the left. The shuttle bus routes also split at Nuorilang, one toward the Virgin Forest and one toward Long Lake. This day was mainly for Rize Valley, which takes the longest of the three.

It was still a weekday, so the entrance line was short. The shuttle's first stop was Shuzheng Lakes. From there we walked uphill past Shuzheng Waterfall and Tiger Lake to Rhinoceros Lake, then caught a bus, planning to go to the Virgin Forest. It turned out to be headed for Long Lake, so we got off at Nuorilang to change buses. The next one didn't go to the Virgin Forest either, and we had to change once more on the way.

It drizzled that day. The lakes were still blue where they were supposed to be, but the sky was a flat gray, and raindrops kept landing on the lens. None of the photos came out well.

The Virgin Forest trail runs uphill, lined with firs. The sign on one Min fir (Abies recurvata) put its age at 460 years. There was no sun up there, and it was chilly. On the trail a family member gave me a piece of ginger candy. I thought it was pretty good, and for the rest of the trip I ate one whenever I got hungry or wanted a snack. From the Virgin Forest we walked all the way down a forest trail along the stream. When we reached Swan Lake at 12:50 p.m., my mom was already saying she was hungry. We kept walking down to the Eagle Claw Cave (Yingzhuadong) stop, then took the bus to Arrow Bamboo Lake.

By 2 p.m. everyone was hungry. At Arrow Bamboo Lake we finally made the instant noodles we had brought, paying CNY 5 extra for hot water, then walked a loop around the lake. Apart from Nuorilang, the only food in the park comes from small stalls along the way selling corn, cookies, and drinks. If you bring your own food, canned eight-treasure congee needs no hot water but is heavy. Most other visitors I saw had rice balls or bread.

In the afternoon we rode past Panda Lake, got off at Five Flower Lake, and walked the boardwalk to Pearl Shoals and Pearl Shoals Waterfall. Before leaving the park, we took the bus down again and got off at Tiger Lake. The weather had been bad when we passed it in the morning, and I had only taken a few quick shots. It was a little better now, so this time I got clear photos of Tiger Lake.

Tibetan Mystery and Dinner

From the park we went straight to Tibetan Mystery, a Tibetan song-and-dance show directed and choreographed by Yang Liping, and made it to the Tibetan Mystery Grand Theatre at 5:30 p.m. I thought the whole show was well worth seeing. Photos are allowed during the performance, but video isn't. Tickets cost less on Ctrip than at the box office, but you have to pick them up at the theater.

The restaurant we had planned on was nowhere to be found when we got there, so we ate highland free-range chicken hot pot at Chen Dachu Nongjia Tucaiguan, a farmhouse-style restaurant. The food was average. Rice was extra: CNY 12, billed as four servings for the five of us.

After dinner we took another car to Lingshi Youming, a discount snack store near the hotel, and bought bread and cookies to eat in the park the next day. We barely touched them.

This time the bus we boarded was bound for the Virgin Forest, the reverse of the day before, so once again we had to get off at Nuorilang and switch to a bus for Long Lake.

Long Lake lies at the far end of Zechawa Valley, the left branch, and at 3,101 m (10,174 ft) it is the highest lake in Jiuzhaigou. There was no rain that day, and it was warmer. The clouds hung low, but the lake was still beautiful. It was very crowded, though, and there is nothing to see besides the lake itself.

From Long Lake we walked down to Five-Color Pond. Its water is paler than in the other lakes and almost seems to glow. Of the lakes that morning, it's the one I remember best.

The bus got us back to Nuorilang right at noon, and we bought fried rice and fried noodles for lunch. In the afternoon we followed Shuzheng Valley downhill, again on a forest trail. We stopped first at Nuorilang Waterfall beside Nuorilang Center, then walked down to the shuttle stop below the falls and rode the bus to Rhinoceros Lake.

When we reached Rhinoceros Lake, the clouds had parted and the sun was out. The lake was calm, and even quick snapshots looked good. I took my best photos of the day there. Farther down we passed a Tibetan prayer wheel. According to the sign beside it, the local Tibetans practice Bon and turn prayer wheels counterclockwise, the opposite of the clockwise direction Tibetan Buddhists use. Below that was Shuzheng Waterfall. The day before we had walked up past it, and this time we passed it on the way down.

Over the two days we saw several waterfalls. My ranking is Pearl Shoals Waterfall first, Shuzheng Waterfall second, and Nuorilang Waterfall third, though all of them are worth seeing. Directly above Pearl Shoals Waterfall is Pearl Shoals, a flat, shallow stretch of the stream. The water runs across it before dropping off the edge as the falls, so I see the shoal and the waterfall as a single feature. Shuzheng Waterfall stands out for its length. The sign at Nuorilang Waterfall lists it as 320 m wide and 24.5 m high and calls it China's widest alpine travertine waterfall.

We passed Bonsai Shoal on the shuttle bus out of the park. We didn't get off, but from the bus it does look like a bonsai display. We left Jiuzhaigou at 3 p.m., and the trip back to Chengdu took nearly six hours. We didn't walk out of Chengdu East Station until 9 p.m.

After checking in at the hotel, we found a Laoma Tihua shop for a late-night snack and ordered a Chengdu staple, pig's trotter soup with white kidney beans.

Panda Valley in Dujiangyan

Most of the people at the hotel breakfast that morning were foreigners. After breakfast, a car and driver we had hired for the whole day took us to the city of Dujiangyan. The first stop was Panda Valley, officially the Dujiangyan Field Research Center for Giant Pandas, run by the Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding. The first giant panda we saw was one named Bao Ge. He was asleep, slumped lazily over a wooden post wrapped in hemp rope. His name sign says as much: he "often likes to drape himself over a high branch for a long nap."

I didn't think pandas were anything special at first, but in person they turned out to be adorably dopey. Most enclosures have only a low fence of wooden posts and no glass, so you can watch the pandas up close as they eat bamboo, sleep, and eat more bamboo. Once we had seen enough, we walked uphill to look for the red pandas and got photos of them climbing around on wooden frames.

We spent just over an hour at Panda Valley. The park has a lot of flowers and plants, all beautifully kept, which reminded me of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, where I'd been on an earlier trip. The park was quiet when we got there. Around 10 a.m., as we were leaving, group after group of local students came in on school field trips, and the place became crowded and chaotic. I'd recommend getting to Panda Valley early. That's also when you can see the pandas eating.

Zhongshuge and Ginkgo Nut Chicken

Zhongshuge, a bookstore chain known for its elaborate interiors, has a branch in Sunac Mall. We went mainly for photos, and a quick look is enough. We had lunch near the old town of Guanxian (Guan County, renamed Dujiangyan in 1988) and ordered chicken stewed with ginkgo nuts (baiguo dunji), a well-known Dujiangyan dish. The soup was very good. The other dishes were ordinary.

Dujiangyan Irrigation System

In the afternoon we entered the Dujiangyan Scenic Area at Qinyan Tower, on Mount Yulei, and hired a guide for a tour of about two hours. From the top of the tower you can see the whole site, with the Anlan Suspension Bridge and the Fish Mouth (Yuzui) right in front of you, though from that far away the Dujiangyan Irrigation System doesn't look like much. The guide started by explaining how Dujiangyan differs from a dam: a dam sits across the river and holds the water back, while Dujiangyan divides the river along its flow and lets the water keep moving downstream. The guide also covered where the name "Dujiangyan" comes from. All I remember is that it had something to do with Chengdu and the Inner River.

We followed the guide downhill from Qinyan Tower, passing the Hall of the God of Wealth and then Erwang Temple (the Two Kings Temple). The temples along this stretch are all Taoist, and Erwang Temple honors the Qin governor Li Bing and his son. After the temples we crossed the Anlan Suspension Bridge. The pavilion at the end of the bridge is inscribed "The World's No. 1 Bridge of Love." The bridge was crowded and swayed a lot as we walked across.

On the other side of the bridge we reached the Fish Mouth, the levee that splits the Min River into the Inner River and the Outer River. The Outer River is the Min's original channel. The Inner River carries water diverted for irrigation and farther down fans out into many smaller channels. The guide said the flow is divided between the two by a "four-six split."

From the Fish Mouth we took the scenic-area shuttle to the Beiting (Stele Pavilion) stop, which saved us a 1 km walk. The shuttle costs CNY 10 one way, but the guide charged us CNY 20 a person, or CNY 100 for the five of us. I felt a little ripped off. Still, the guide was friendly and knowledgeable, so I counted the difference as a tip. There may also have been a misunderstanding somewhere.

The Beiting stop is right next to Feishayan, the Flying Sand Weir, which sends excess water, sand, and gravel from the Inner River back into the Outer River. We continued down to Baopingkou, the Bottle-Neck Channel cut through Mount Yulei, where the Inner River enters the Chengdu Plain. The small hill the cut separated from the mountain is called Lidui. Here the guide told the legend behind Fulong Temple (the Dragon-Taming Temple): an evil dragon that had been causing trouble was chained in Fulong Pool beneath Lidui. The words "A Dragon Subdued in the Cold Pool" are carved into the Lidui cliff. The guide then described a measuring device and said we would see replicas of it near the end.

We finished by walking Yangong Road to the South Gate exit. The road is lined with 12 statues of people who built and maintained the waterworks over the centuries, and water runs in a roadside channel close enough to touch. On this last stretch we also saw the replicas of the measuring device the guide had described: a few columns lying on their sides. At the South Gate exit there's a marker stone carved with "World Heritage · Dujiangyan," and we took a photo with it to end the visit.

After listening to the guide the whole way, I came to see Dujiangyan as an impressive piece of hydraulic engineering that works differently from a reservoir. According to the guide, Du Fu and other poets had written about Dujiangyan, which gave me a sense of how much history the place has. If you want to understand Dujiangyan, hire a guide. You'll notice a lot more.

Right outside the South Gate is Nanqiao Bridge, a covered bridge over the Inner River. Beside it is a pedestrian street where you can snack as you walk, and it's a pleasant place to wander. There's also an old city gate nearby. Most of Guanxian's city wall has been torn down, and this is the only one of its original gates still standing. I later found out it's Xuanwei Gate, on West Street (Xijie).

Background: Dujiangyan's Origins and How It Works

Dujiangyan diverts water from the river without damming it. Taiwan's Water Resources Agency defines both dams and weirs as structures built across a river to hold back water. A tall structure, or one where floodwater spills over only part of the crest, is a dam. A low structure, or one that water can spill over along its entire crest, is a weir. The agency adds that there is no clear line between the two. The Flying Sand Weir lets surplus water from the Inner River spill over its full crest back into the Outer River, which fits the definition of a weir.

The most common explanation of the name is that "Dujiang" is short for "Chengdu River." Li Xian, a Tang dynasty crown prince, wrote in his commentary on the Book of the Later Han: "Dujiang is the Chengdu River." The Kuodi Zhi, a Tang-era geography, says the Pi River, which flowed through Chengdu, was also called the Chengdu River, the Middle River, and the Inner River. That is probably the connection to Chengdu and the Inner River that the guide described. But the Inner River of the old texts is the Pi River through Chengdu, a different channel from the one that branches off at the Fish Mouth today.

Li Bing, Qin's governor of Shu (the western Sichuan Basin around Chengdu) in the late Warring States period, is a documented historical figure. "The Treatise on the Yellow River and Canals" in the Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian) says: "Bing, governor of Shu, cut through Lidui... and dug two channels through Chengdu." There is no such record for his son, Erlang. The story of a "son of Li Bing" who helped tame the river did not spread until the Northern Song dynasty. When the Southern Song writer Fan Chengda described Fulong Temple in Diary of a Boat Trip to Wu (Wuchuan lu), he wrote: "Legend has it that Governor Li chained an evil dragon beneath Lidui." Governor Li here means Li Bing.

As for the "four-six split" the guide mentioned, China's Ministry of Water Resources explains that in the dry season about 60 percent of the water enters the Inner River for irrigation, and in flood season about 60 percent goes down the Outer River to carry off the floodwater. An old mnemonic sums it up: "Split it four to six, and flood and drought even out." The ratio in the mnemonic is approximate rather than measured. The Sichuan Dujiangyan Water Conservancy Development Center, which runs the waterworks, says an Outer River sluice gate was built in 1974 and that water is now allocated according to what the irrigation district needs.

From what I found later, the measuring device the guide described was most likely the wotie, iron bars buried under the Inner River's bed at Fengqiwo as depth markers. During the annual winter maintenance, crews dredge the riverbed until the wotie are exposed, the sign that they have dug deep enough. The originals are still buried in the river. The four bars displayed in a fountain in the scenic area are replicas, and they are probably the ones we saw at the end of our walk.

Kuanzhai Alley and Tianfu Square

After Dujiangyan, we rode a little over an hour back to Chengdu and went to Kuanzhai Alley (the "Wide and Narrow Alleys"), a shopping street. It didn't rain that night, and we took it easy, browsing and snacking as we went. We also bought two drinks at Starbucks. Dinner was at the Kuanzhai Alley branch of Meizhou Dongpo, a Sichuan restaurant chain. The food was good and cheap.

Some of the family were tired, so we called a car to take them back to the hotel. The rest of us took the metro to Tianfu Square: Line 4 from Kuanzhaixiangzi Alleys Station, then a transfer to Line 1 at Luomashi, just two stops in all. The square is huge, but there wasn't much left to see that late. We took a few photos and walked back to the hotel.

Wuhou Shrine

In the morning we left our luggage at the hotel and went to the Chengdu Wuhou Shrine Museum, where the two seniors got in free. We didn't hire a guide this time and used an audio guide instead. I later decided you don't really need the audio guide. When something didn't make sense, I asked the AI in Alibaba's Qwen app. For example, I wanted to know how Zhuge Liang's two memorials to the throne, the Former and Later Chu Shi Biao, differ and who the "Biography of the Former Lord" and the "Biography of the Later Lord" in the Records of the Three Kingdoms are each about. The AI answered faster than the audio guide, which makes it handy for museums in China.

At the Wuhou Shrine, the main things to see are statues of Three Kingdoms figures such as Liu Bei, Zhuge Liang, and Zhang Fei. Liu Bei and Zhuge Liang are honored together here, and the plaque in the passage hall reads "Xianzhu Wuhou tong bi gong," meaning the Former Lord (Liu Bei) and Marquis Wu (Zhuge Liang) share one shrine. Liu Bei's son, Liu Shan, is often written off with Confucius's line "Rotten wood cannot be carved." In the hall, right next to the panel about Liu Shan, sits a tree stump with its roots still attached. I assume it's a jab at him, and I found the placement amusing.

The exhibition also covers how the shrine changed over the dynasties. A display wall titled "Tianxia Wuhou" maps Wuhou shrines in other places, and even Nantou County in Taiwan is marked on it.

From there we passed Sanyi Temple, dedicated to the sworn brothers Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei, and ended at Hui Mausoleum, Liu Bei's tomb. Just outside is the shopping area around Jinli Ancient Street, which we skipped this time. I spent most of my time at the shrine looking at statues and taking photos, and it was my favorite stop of the day. I went through it slowly, probably because I had the AI to answer my questions.

Taode Shaguo

Lunch was at Taode Shaguo, a Sichuan clay-pot chain. The food was good and not expensive. I'd reserve a table in advance. My top recommendation is the garlic shrimp (CNY 56). The clay pot heats on a tabletop butane burner with an hourglass beside it, and the shrimp is at its best if you eat it before the sand runs out. We also ordered a Kung Pao chicken clay pot and pig's trotters braised with soybeans.

Chunxi Road, Yonghui Supermarket, and Wenshu Monastery

In the afternoon we went to the Chunxi Road pedestrian street. On the way, a family member noticed a phone had been left behind at Taode Shaguo. My mom took a car back by herself to get it while the rest of us kept browsing nearby. The giant panda climbing the outside of the Chengdu IFS mall is an art installation, I Am Here by Lawrence Argent, and I think it's worth a look. Apart from that, the whole street is lined with shops, and the nearby Daci Temple didn't do much for me.

Later that afternoon we took a car to a Yonghui supermarket at New City Plaza to look around. Fruit there is fairly cheap. The shelf label called the Hami melons "Tengyu," at CNY 17.98 per kilogram. A half melon weighing 0.98 kg cost CNY 17.62, about TWD 80. We didn't buy one because the hotel breakfast had Hami melon every day. The melon there was really good, and I had some almost every morning.

This trip was also my first time eating winter jujubes (dongzao). We bought some at the supermarket, and later the hotel breakfast had them too. They were good. Each one is a single crunchy bite, though I didn't find them very sweet. Taiwanese jujubes are much bigger, and you have to bite your way around them. The flesh sometimes gets stuck between your teeth, but they're sweeter. I looked it up after the trip: winter jujube is a cultivar of the Chinese jujube (Ziziphus jujuba), the species that gets dried into red dates. The jujube commonly eaten in Taiwan is the Indian jujube (Z. mauritiana), in the same genus as winter jujube but a different species.

Outside the supermarket was a CNY 10 express haircut stand. A family member got a haircut there, so we can say we've tried one in China. From the supermarket we took a car to Wenshu Monastery. The monastery didn't leave much of an impression on me, and I was out in 20 minutes, though my mom did buy a few things.

My parents were tired and returned to the hotel early. The other three of us walked to Gongting Gaodianpu, a traditional pastry shop near Wenshu Monastery, and bought palace-style milk cakes, Suzhou-style pork floss pastries, a napoleon, and pumpkin-seed pastries, CNY 53 in total. At Dongzikou Zhang Lao'er Liangfen, a Chengdu jelly-noodle and snack shop, we also got chilled laozao (sweet fermented rice) and bingfen (Sichuan ice jelly) with brown sugar syrup and taro balls. Neither was anything special. Only the pastries from Gongting Gaodianpu were good enough to recommend.

Buying a Day Tour at the Chongqing Scenic Area Booking Center

After going back to the hotel for our bags, we took the high-speed train from Chengdu East Station to Chongqing's Shapingba Railway Station. We had booked a car to meet us at the station, but we still waited half an hour before we could get in. On the way, the driver suggested we stop at the "Chongqing Scenic Area Booking Center." There we bought a day tour for the next day, a package sold as "Bayu Street Life & Viral Hotspots," listed at CNY 168 and discounted to CNY 138. It covered about the same sights we had planned and came with a guide, which at the time seemed like less hassle. We also hadn't bought tickets for the Yangtze River Cableway yet and assumed they needed a reservation. A tour meant we wouldn't have to hail taxis or walk long distances. We had no idea this was the start of a nightmare.

We checked in at TRYP by Wyndham Chongqing Downtown in Jiefangbei, the business district around the People's Liberation Monument. The front desk had to register our Taiwan compatriot permits. Because it was our last stop, we had all our online orders shipped there, and the hotel received 20 or 30 packages for us. It was the best hotel of the trip. The room had welcome fruit on the desk and free drinks in the minibar, and the metro station is close by. The downside was that it had only two elevators, and we often had to wait.

Hongyadong at Night and Qiu Niangniang Hot Pot

We dropped off our bags and went straight to Hongyadong (Hongya Cave). It was the best view we had in Chongqing: the sky was clear, and the whole row of stilt houses (diaojiaolou) was lit up in gold. The place was packed, and nearly every car on the road was a taxi. We walked onto Qiansimen Bridge over the Jialing River to look at Hongyadong. I took this post's cover photo from the bridge.

Dinner wasn't until after 11 p.m., at Qiu Niangniang Hot Pot. We had Chongqing mala (numbing and spicy) hot pot. The red broth is built on beef tallow, so you're essentially cooking in oil. Drops that landed on the table cooled into white lumps, which was a little unsettling to see. The hot pot was very spicy, and a group at the next table kept getting louder over some game. It was an uncomfortable meal, and it was already midnight when we finished.

Baixiangju and the Great Hall of the People

The first stop was Baixiangju, a 1990s apartment complex set into the hillside, with entrances on the 1st, 10th, and 15th floors. It's now a popular photo spot. From the top you can watch Yangtze River Cableway cars cross the river. The cableway is visible from many places in Chongqing, though, so I don't think you miss anything by skipping Baixiangju.

The second stop was the Chongqing Great Hall of the People. The municipal government is directly across from the hall, and there's a museum nearby. I think the hall is worth seeing, but this time we left once we had our photos.

Chen Mahua City Showroom: A CNY 6,774 Bag of Fritillary Powder

A little after 10 a.m., the guide took us to the Chen Mahua City Showroom. Chen Mahua is a Chongqing brand of fried dough twists, and the showroom's promotional material says it focuses on Chongqing's intangible cultural heritage and signature local products. A jar of fritillary bulbs (beimu), a Chinese herbal medicine, was on display. A family member saw others in our group buying it and asked a clerk, who said it was chuanbei (Sichuan fritillary bulb). Without consulting the rest of us, that family member decided to buy some and agreed on a price of CNY 6 per gram, assuming that was the going rate for chuanbei. None of us had researched prices beforehand. The clerk ground the bulbs into powder without weighing them or quoting a total first, and wrote up the receipt only after grinding: 1,129 grams, CNY 6,774 in total. We later returned it, and I cover how the return went further down.

Liziba: A Gold Vault and a Monorail Through a Building

The Gold Vault and Currency Museum at Liziba wasn't included in the package: admission is CNY 58, and the agency charged us CNY 68. The exhibits cover gold vaults and banknotes from the Republican era (1912–1949). The guided commentary only skimmed each topic, so to understand anything you have to read the signs yourself. The real point comes at the end, with a sales pitch for treasure bowls (jubaopen), feng shui charms said to multiply wealth. I don't think the vault is worth paying extra for.

From there we went to the viewing area at Liziba Station, where monorail trains on Chongqing Rail Transit Line 2 pass through a residential building. The platform was packed and hot, and someone was smoking. We waited for a train to emerge from the building, took our photos, and were back on the bus in about five minutes.

Ciqikou: A Group Lunch and a Fortune-Telling Pitch

Lunch was at Lao Sichuan Restaurant next to Ciqikou Ancient Town: a group meal of ten dishes and a soup for CNY 35 per person. It tasted like staff cafeteria food. The pumpkin soup was just pumpkin and water, with no flavor at all.

At Ciqikou, some people in the group paid extra to see Sichuan opera. We didn't, and the guide took us to Wanxiang Pavilion (Amap, the Chinese map app, labels the same spot Wenchang Pavilion), saying a guided tour had been arranged for us. According to the guide, the building originally honored Wenchang Dijun, the Taoist god of literature and examinations, and was rebuilt after being bombed by the Japanese. It now enshrines Confucius. A statue of Confucius stands just inside the entrance, with red ribbons in front of it. Visitors are supposed to write their name, where their home's feng shui wealth corner is, and other details on a ribbon. It reminded me a little of the free keepsakes Taiwanese temples hand out to form a karmic bond (jieyuan). The presenter at the pavilion said they basically don't let students take these ribbons. My guess is that students don't have much money and aren't going to come here asking about wealth corners. Behind the Confucius statue, the talk covered wealth corners and feng shui. Farther in, it moved on to the bagua (eight trigrams) and the I Ching (Book of Changes). The presenter also told us that none of us knew where the wealth corner in our own home was.

At the end we were led to a group discussion area, where the 30-plus people on the tour were divided into cubicles, each with a "teacher" sitting inside. The teacher first asked for our birth years and Chinese zodiac signs, then claimed that the married couple in our family weren't getting along and tended to argue, and finally pitched products at CNY 100, 300, and 500. When we didn't buy anything, the teacher's attitude changed at once, and there was no more talk of writing down where our wealth corner was. They use the names of Confucius and a historic building to sell things. I found it worse than the same kind of scheme in Taiwan. Most places like this have hidden charges.

After that we had free time on Ciqikou's back streets. They weren't crowded, and most of the shops were chains. The guide said Ciqikou used to be much smaller: the commercial area keeps expanding, and streets that once carried cars are now pedestrian-only. Farther on are the narrower old lanes that climb up and down.

I didn't like Ciqikou. Skip it if you can.

Fact Check: Wanxiang Pavilion and Wenchang Pavilion

When I checked after the trip, only part of the guide's story held up. Historically, Ciqikou's Wenchang Palace was a Taoist temple. Most of its buildings were destroyed by bombing during the Second Sino-Japanese War, and all that remains is the ruin of the old fortress gate by the river on Jinbi Zhengjie. That part matches the guide's bombing story. But the Wenchang Pavilion we visited is on Nanjie (South Street), and I found no source saying it was rebuilt on Wenchang Palace's original site. Online travel guides say Wenchang Pavilion enshrines Wenchang Dijun, yet the statue we saw inside the entrance was Confucius. The tour itinerary lists "Wanxiang Pavilion," and Amap marks the same spot as Wenchang Pavilion. Beyond that, I couldn't find anything online linking the two names.

In July 2025, a woman filed a complaint on Xiaofeibao, a Chinese consumer-complaint platform, about a similar experience. A "Master Zhou" at Wenchang Pavilion had told her she was "clashing with Tai Sui," the year's ruling deity, and needed to pay to ward off bad luck. The amount in dispute was CNY 399. The next day she closed it herself, noting that she had been refunded. Fortune-telling at Ciqikou isn't a new problem either. In 2018, CQNEWS (Hualong Net), Chongqing's news portal, reported on a crackdown the Ciqikou Ancient Town management committee had launched against fortune-telling and character divination.

Two Rivers Ferry

In the afternoon we went back to Hongyadong and took the Two Rivers Ferry from the Hongyadong Passenger Terminal across the Yangtze to Danzishi. Our original plan was a boat from Ciqikou to Hongyadong, which I remember as about an hour on the water. The package put us on this short run instead. The boat goes from the Jialing River into the Yangtze and docks about 10 minutes after leaving. As soon as we boarded, we found a stall selling sliced fruit at CNY 10 a plate. The upper deck had a VIP area that charged another CNY 10 for self-serve tea. It felt like buying a first-class seat on a high-speed train and then being asked to pay again for an upgrade once on board.

Cloud Eye Tickets and the Elevator Fee

Back in Jiefangbei, the guide described the Cloud Eye Observation Deck and the Yangtze River Cableway as "free extras," which made skipping them sound like no loss. But both stops were printed right on the package itinerary, with admission listed, so skipping them would only save the tour company money. The guide also kept saying we would wait an hour in line for the cableway at night, which sounded like an attempt to talk us out of it.

The contract framed it the same way: "CNY 88 Cloud Eye experience, free for you (CNY 10 elevator fee not included)." Anyone going up had to transfer the elevator fee to the guide while still on the bus. Only three of us went up, while my mom and another family member went to eat and rest. At the deck, though, we saw other visitors buy a ticket and go straight up without paying any separate elevator fee.

Chongqing was foggy on most of the days we were there. The fog usually thinned a little around midday, and sometimes even more toward evening. When we went up a little after 5 p.m., it was still daylight and the view was only so-so. It would probably look better at night, but from that height we could still see the confluence of the Yangtze and Jialing rivers and the whole city. The deck also offers a "free photo," but the free version is only a one-inch print. A regular-size print costs CNY 20. We left with just the one-inch photo, and it wasn't a good shot anyway.

Yangtze River Cableway and Reservations

We went back to the hotel to rest in the early evening. That night a different guide took us to the cableway's south station, and we boarded after about half an hour, shorter than the hour the day-tour guide had warned us about. On paper, the cableway requires buying a ticket and reserving in advance. But that day was a Monday right after the Mid-Autumn Festival holiday, and the station wasn't crowded. We watched independent travelers come up the elevator and scan a QR code to join the line. On weekdays you probably don't need to reserve. If you plan to ride the cableway, avoiding holidays will make it much easier. I thought the cableway was the most beautiful stop of the day. We rode from the south station to the north station, and I filmed the entire crossing, with the Chongqing night skyline outside the window. The north station is right in Jiefangbei. We walked back to the hotel from there and passed the busy Chongqing Times Square mall on the way. Dinner was a quick meal of xiaolongbao (soup dumplings) and a non-spicy bowl of Chongqing xiaomian.

Breakfast and Airport Luggage Delivery

The last hotel breakfast included bobo chicken, which I had wanted to try for a while. It's a cold dish: cooked tofu skin and wood ear mushrooms on bamboo skewers, left to cool and soaked in either a mala sauce or a jiaoma sauce made with green Sichuan pepper. It was just okay. The rest of the breakfast at the TRYP was very good.

The hotel offers luggage delivery to the airport. You leave your bags at the front desk in the morning and go out for the day without having to come back for them. That let us cancel the seven-seat airport transfer we had booked (CNY 93) at no charge. In the afternoon we took two ride-hailing cars to the airport instead. One had a fixed upfront fare of CNY 23, and the other was metered at CNY 41. The two fares differed by almost CNY 20, but together they still cost less than the seven-seater. Our suitcases were big and a hassle to drag around, so on future trips I'll make airport luggage delivery a priority when I book the last night's hotel.

Mountain City Alley

In the morning we went to Mountain City Alley (Shancheng Xiang), which I liked a lot, far more than Ciqikou. The lanes have an old-town look, with some trendy little shops and a Catholic church. The best part was the cats. We had barely started walking when a cat came out to greet us. Ragdolls and black-and-white cats wandered in and out of the shops. One ran upstairs, and a clerk had to go up and carry it back down. The place reminded me a little of Houtong, Taiwan's cat village, except that the cats here keep mostly to the shops.

Partway through we stopped at Shazi Xiangxiang Tangyuan, a shop selling tangyuan (glutinous rice balls). Signs out front claim intangible cultural heritage status and "5 appearances on CCTV" (China's state broadcaster). A bowl of large tangyuan in lotus seed soup cost CNY 18. Split among four people, it came to one bite each. For two people it would be plenty. The tangyuan tasted good and photographed well.

We walked up to the viewing platform at the top of the hill, which looks out over the Yangtze and the Nanjimen rail bridge. Farther down, a shop displays live wood ear mushrooms. We sat down there for a wood ear and lotus seed drink and left without buying anything.

Shibati and the Air-Raid Shelter

From Mountain City Alley we walked all the way to Shibati (Eighteen Steps), a restored historic district. I think we came in through a back lane. There was hardly anyone around at first, and the crowds grew as we got closer to the main stairway. Going up and down the stone steps is tiring, but there is an escalator that runs from the bottom all the way up to Ancient Well Square. With high-rises on every side, the scenery isn't much unless you shoot from a drone.

The Shibati Memories Museum mainly covers the area's history and also gives you a quick look at Shibati as a whole. There's a lot of material, and I didn't get much out of it. A quick walk-through is enough. The museum is air-conditioned and cool, though, which makes it a good place to rest. I'd still recommend stopping in.

My mom rested on the top platform while the rest of us took the escalator down to find the Great Tunnel, a wartime air-raid shelter. It was hot inside, and our phones had no signal at all. An exhibition titled The More You Bomb, the Stronger We Are covers the Japanese bombing of Chongqing during the war. The Great Tunnel Massacre took place right here on June 5, 1941. Chongqing still has air-raid shelters everywhere, left over from those years.

Deng Bandeng Noodles

We had lunch at Deng Bandeng Noodles near Shibati. The signature wanza noodles, topped with peas and minced pork, cost CNY 16. I had the house special combo noodles for CNY 35, a very good bowl with pig's trotter, beef, pork intestines, and beef tendon.

Jiangbei Airport and the Trip Home

After lunch we took cars straight to Terminal 3 at Chongqing Jiangbei International Airport, where we collected the bags the hotel had sent ahead. Check-in and departure immigration went mostly smoothly. The one holdup was a small power bank my mom had packed in her checked bag, which took airport staff 15 to 30 minutes to locate. Oddly, on arrival we had been put in the same line as mainland Chinese travelers, but on departure, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan flights were grouped with international ones.

An Americano from N23 Coffee in the airport 7-Eleven cost CNY 18.9, which is pretty steep. The airport also sells most of the local specialties people take home as gifts, though prices may run a little higher than in the city.

On the way back, CX945 reached Hong Kong around 7:30 p.m. The meal on board was good, and they served beer. We connected to CX448 in Hong Kong and landed in Kaohsiung at 11 p.m., then waited a long time at baggage claim.

Returning the Beimu

We bought the beimu at the Chen Mahua City Showroom on September 28. By early evening, the more I thought about the purchase, the stranger it seemed. Luckily, I had taken photos in the store as evidence. When I checked the photo of the price label, I noticed for the first time that it gave the origin as Heilongjiang and the product name as "beimu (sold loose by weight)," Grade 1. The receipt also said only "beimu," yet the clerk had told us it was chuanbei. I asked Qwen and Claude. Claude said it was pingbeimu (Ussuri fritillary, Fritillaria ussuriensis) and that it sells for less than CNY 6 a gram. My rough estimate that evening put the powder at only about CNY 150. I decided we had to find a way to return it and raised it with the family member who had made the purchase. We agreed to deal with it after the day tour.

We first reported the problem through Alipay and were advised to call 12345, the government service hotline. We did call, because we no longer trusted the store. In practice, though, the simplest approach is to ask the seller directly whether you can return the item. If the seller agrees, get your money back first, and call 12345 only if the seller refuses. The family member who bought the powder called the seller and explained the situation. The seller kept trying to deceive us, insisting the product was genuine, and only at the end said we could return it within 30 days. The family member then added the seller on WeChat. We handled the return over lunch the next day at Deng Bandeng Noodles. It was our first time using DiDi's on-demand courier service, which picks up a package from wherever you are and delivers it. We booked it without leaving the noodle shop. A courier came in, took the package, photographed it, and delivered it straight to the seller. The delivery cost CNY 17. The seller refunded us over WeChat as soon as the package arrived.

Later, while we were going through exit immigration and security at Jiangbei Airport, an official from Chongqing's Yuzhong District called to check on us and reminded us not to buy things carelessly when traveling. My guess is that even the local authorities don't trust these shops much.

Fact Check: Chuanbei and Pingbeimu

Under the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China (the Chinese Pharmacopoeia), chuanbei comes from six plant sources: Fritillaria cirrhosa, F. unibracteata, F. przewalskii, F. delavayi, F. taipaiensis, and F. unibracteata var. wabuensis. Pingbeimu is not among them. Heilongjiang, the origin on our label, is the main growing region for pingbeimu, with more than 70 percent of China's output.

The two are far apart in wholesale price. According to 2026 quotes on zyctd.com, a Chinese herbal-medicine price site, pingbeimu sold for about CNY 52 to 130 per kilogram in July, or roughly CNY 0.05 to 0.13 per gram. Songbei, the top commercial grade of chuanbei, was about CNY 3,200 to 3,600 per kilogram in September. At CNY 6 a gram, the price we paid was 1.7 to 1.9 times the songbei wholesale price. That doesn't sound outrageous for chuanbei, which makes it easy to fool buyers. If what we bought was pingbeimu, we paid more than 46 times the wholesale price: our 1,129 grams would have cost only about CNY 59 to 147 wholesale. All of these wholesale prices are for whole bulbs and don't include grinding or retail markup.

Passing off pingbeimu as chuanbei is a long-standing problem in the Chinese herbal medicine trade. Hong Kong's Department of Health has found herbal soup packs that labeled pingbeimu as chuanbei. Once ground into powder, the two look the same, and telling them apart takes microscopic identification or DNA testing. We never had our powder tested, so we can't say for sure which beimu it was.

Chengdu or Chongqing: Which Is More Fun?

The best parts of this trip were Jiuzhaigou, Dujiangyan, and Panda Valley, and Jiuzhaigou in particular is worth a visit. Panda Valley was my favorite place around Chengdu, and within the city I liked the Wuhou Shrine. Kuanzhai Alley is a shopping street with places to eat and browse, much like a Taiwanese night market or Taipei's Ximending. I don't see it as one of those viral spots people visit only for photos.

Next to those three, downtown Chongqing was the boring part of the trip, except for Mountain City Alley and Hongyadong at night. The boring stops were all on the day tour: the gold vault museum, the monorail running through a building at Liziba, Wanxiang Pavilion, the Two Rivers Ferry, and Cloud Eye. Every one of them except the monorail came with hidden charges, another mark against sightseeing in downtown Chongqing. Chongqing's main appeal is the terrain behind its Mountain City nickname, with buildings set high on steep slopes. I suspect many old buildings were lost in the Japanese bombing and that most of what looks old today is rebuilt.

We didn't spend long in Chongqing and skipped several major sights, but if I planned the trip again, I would still trim the time in downtown Chongqing. Trips outside the city might suit us better than these viral photo spots.

Lessons from the traps we fell into on this trip:

  • Don't buy local specialties on impulse. At a shopping stop there's no market price to compare against, and the shop may not quote a total up front. That is how one bag of beimu powder cost us CNY 6,774.
  • Ask for the price before you pay, including the total and any extra charges. The Dujiangyan shuttle costs CNY 10 one way, but our guide charged CNY 20 per person. Some DiDi drivers also asked for an extra fee when five of us squeezed into one car.
  • Don't join tour groups. The driver who picked us up at the station recommended the Chongqing day tour. It included a shopping stop, and the guide called attractions printed on the itinerary "free extras." Tour groups that depart from Taiwan also hand some stops over to local guides once they arrive. Plan the sights yourself and buy tickets through official channels.
  • Set up Alipay or WeChat Pay before you go. You'll need it for DiDi rides, ticket reservations, payments, and refunds.
  • If you get ripped off, save evidence first: photograph the price label and the receipt. Then request a return through Alipay or the 12345 hotline, or contact the seller directly.
