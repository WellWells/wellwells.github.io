Chunghwa Telecom Fiber Upgrade: 100M/40M to Symmetric 300M/300M

My student plan expired after more than a decade, so I moved the line up to 300M/300M. What the 300M, 500M and 1G tiers cost, where the tier-jump bonus threshold sits, the downgrade fee buried in the contract, and what happened to my static IP.

Introduction

I have been on Chunghwa Telecom's fiber for more than a decade, always at 100M/40M. The student plan billed annually at TWD 7,830, which works out to TWD 652.5 a month. The promotional period ran to 2026/08/19 and the account page showed the contract as complete.

At that price it was a good deal, so I left it alone for years. A completed contract doesn't force your hand either. You can keep using the line without renewing anything.

What finally pushed me was the upload. Downloads at 100M rarely bottleneck at home, but 40M up makes backups and remote connections noticeably worse. Since the contract had just run out, I took another look at what was on offer.

I ended up on 300M/300M.

Taiwan Home Broadband Runs on Only Two Physical Networks: Chunghwa Telecom and Cable TV

Before settling on a speed I compared plans and pricing across a few providers, and there turned out to be fewer real choices than the brand names suggest.

There are really only two physical networks here. One is the fiber Chunghwa Telecom runs from its exchange to your home, sold as HiNet Fiber. The other belongs to the local cable-TV operator, the same line that used to carry television channels and now sells internet access as well.

Plenty of brands sit on top of those two. FarEasTone Broadband and So-net lease Chunghwa Telecom's fiber and replace only the service layer, so quality and coverage match Chunghwa Telecom exactly, because it is physically the same line. It also works the other way around, where the bill carries a carrier's name while the local cable operator runs the drop to your door and maintains it, over the cable-TV network.

The cable side has no nationwide operator. Each region is served by its own system operator, and the names are less familiar than the telcos:

  • Home+ (China Network Systems): all of Keelung, Taipei except Shilin and Beitou, New Taipei, northern Taoyuan, Tainan and Kaohsiung.
  • kbro (Fubon group): Taipei, New Taipei, Taoyuan, Hsinchu City, Taichung, Changhua, northern Tainan and northern Pingtung.
  • TWM Broadband (Taiwan Fixed Network Media): New Taipei, Yilan, Hualien and the Fengshan area of Kaohsiung.
  • TBC, Taiwan Broadband Communications: southern Taoyuan, Hsinchu County, Miaoli and central Taichung.
  • Taiwan Optical Platform (台數科, brands 哈TV+ and 哈NET): the fifth-largest operator group nationwide and the widest coverage in central and southern Taiwan, taking in the Datun and west-coast districts of Taichung, Nantou, Yunlin, coastal Chiayi and southern Tainan. In central Taichung it sells internet only, since cable TV there belongs to another operator.
  • Dada Broadband (a brand shared by Dafeng Media and TBC): southwestern New Taipei around Banqiao and Zhonghe, Taoyuan, Hsinchu County and City, Miaoli, Taichung and Kaohsiung.
  • Taipei Net: all districts of Taipei City.

So the real decision is which physical network you sit on, not which brand prints the invoice. What you can actually sign up for depends on your district. Every operator has a coverage lookup on its own site, and entering your address tells you whether the line reaches your door, because even within one district it often only reaches part of the area. Both were available where I live, so I still had to pick one.

I went with Chunghwa Telecom. Low latency and symmetric bandwidth were the main reasons, and you can request a static IP if you need one, which helps when something breaks. More on the static IP later, because after actually going through the process my understanding of it changed.

Choosing a Tier: 300M, 500M, or 1G

Upgrading and Renewing Online

Log in to the Chunghwa Telecom site and open the plan selection page. It lists the contracts under your name and the plans you can move to. The current HiNet Fiber lineup is marketed as 速在必行, and upgrades, renewals and new applications are all chosen from this page.

You can sign a new contract up to a month before the old one expires. It doesn't take effect immediately, it queues behind the existing contract, so handling it 30 days early leaves no gap and no overlap.

After that it's comparing rates. Some new-subscriber plans come with points but also a TWD 500 circuit connection fee. Renewals and upgrades don't carry that fee.

All three cards show upgrade and renewal pricing, which is a separate set of numbers from new applications.

Renewing at the same speed costs more than my old student plan, so staying at 100M saves nothing. Since every option meant paying more, I might as well fix the upload while I was at it.

Hami Points: Nothing for a Renewal, Something for a Tier Jump

The official site splits the rewards into online-store exclusive, new-customer bonus, limited-time bonus and VIP bonus, which looks generous until you notice that renewing at the same speed earns nothing at all. Points go to new subscribers and to existing customers who jump a tier, and those thresholds are tighter than you would expect.

I was on 100M/40M, just outside the threshold for 300M, so the 600-point tier-jump bonus didn't apply and I was left with the 300-point limited-time bonus. Only 500M or 1G would have counted as a jump. VIP customers get another 200 points at 500M and above, which needs a six-month average of TWD 1,500 across mobile, HiNet broadband, MOD and landline billing, so I left it out of my numbers.

Put the last two columns side by side and new subscribers are clearly treated better. At 300M that is 1,800 more points, and even after the TWD 500 circuit connection fee they come out TWD 1,300 ahead. The definition is strict, though. Neither your ID number nor the installation address may currently have broadband service, the address must carry no termination record within the past 30 days, and putting it in a family member's name doesn't help either. Collecting those points means terminating first and leaving the address offline for a full month, which most people won't do.

300M vs 500M Is Really a TWD 75 Difference

The monthly gap between 300M and 500M is TWD 100 and the points gap is 600. Hami Points come off the bill at 1 point to TWD 1, so 600 points spread across 24 months works out to TWD 25 a month. That puts the real difference at TWD 75.

TWD 75 for double the bandwidth looks good on paper. But there aren't many people in the house, 300M/300M covers what we actually use, and the extra headroom at 500M would sit idle.

1G was easier to rule out. It costs TWD 300 a month more than 300M without adding any points over 500M, and only 1G and above is subject to the daily traffic cap. Three consecutive days over 150GB combined up and down and you get pushed back to 300M on days five and six.

The Wi-Fi Mesh Unit Included in the Plan

The student plan came with a modem only, so the wireless side was on me. The new plan includes one Wi-Fi mesh unit at no extra charge.

A single unit only provides Wi-Fi access. Two or more of the same model on one circuit are what forms a mesh with roaming inside the coverage area, and extra units can be rented for a larger home. Asking to change the model or spec during the term costs a Wi-Fi subsidy clawback plus a TWD 250 installation and setup fee, and which spec and model you get is a choice the contract reserves for Chunghwa Telecom.

Mine is an Arcadyan WE410443-TC. Checking the spec, it turns out to be Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) 4T4R. 802.11ac was ratified in 2013, Wi-Fi 6 has been common since 2019, and Wi-Fi 7 has been on the market for two years now. Handing out Wi-Fi 5 as a plan freebie in 2026 is, put kindly, included equipment. Put less kindly, it's clearing old stock. Chunghwa Telecom does have a Wi-Fi 6 model, the WG630223-TC, but which one shows up at your door comes down to that reserved-choice clause, and the customer has no say in it.

The Chunghwa Telecom logo stamped on the case is also oversized and ugly. I never plugged it in. The house already runs OpenWRT, set up the way I want it.

If you actually want a mesh, buying retail units works out cheaper than renting them. You pick the model yourself, and nothing is tied to the contract term.

The equipment belongs to Chunghwa Telecom and goes back with the modem when you terminate.

Contract Details: Downgrade Fee and Early-Termination Clawback

The figures below come from the contract I agreed to in August 2026. Chunghwa Telecom reserves the right to change the terms, so go by whatever version appears on your own screen.

Upgrading costs nothing, but once you are on the higher tier, moving back down to a cheaper plan brings a HiNet and circuit change fee of TWD 400. The likeliest time to run into it is month 25, when the promotional pricing ends and the list rate takes over, which is exactly when dropping a tier to save money starts to look attractive.

If you pick the wrong speed, you have 7 days from completion to ask to go back to your original one, with no change fee and the old plan's billing for that period. Past 7 days you are treated as accepting the new speed, and the same ID number or address can only use this once every 180 days.

Early termination is prorated by day, with the contract splitting two years into 730 days and billing back whatever you haven't used. The table below is that daily clawback rate, not a monthly charge:

Installation and What Came After

Installation Day

The appointment was for 9:00 and the technician turned up at 8:40. I was still washing up, so a family member let him in.

The wall only had an old telephone socket and no fiber at all, so a new line had to be run in from outside.

He brought a full cable reel with him. Pulling the fiber took about 10 minutes, the rest of the time went on speed testing, and he was done by 9:15. The connection was only actually down for those 10 minutes.

The jacket is printed Chunghwa Telecom 2C-FL-NSS-BIBSM Fiber TAITUNG. Breaking that down:

CodeMeaning
2C2 cores. GPON sends and receives on a single core, the other is a spare
FLFlat, a flat cable roughly 3.1 × 2.0 mm in cross-section
NSSNon-self-supporting, no built-in messenger wire, so it needs hangers or clips
BIBSMBend-insensitive broadband single-mode, matching ITU-T G.657.A1, usable across 1260 to 1625 nm
TAITUNGTai Tung Communication, one of Chunghwa Telecom's cable suppliers

The modem is a PG004, the white box Taiwanese users nickname the little turtle (小烏龜).

Before packing up, the technician runs a speed test on the spot, just a speed-test site to confirm the numbers land, and then asks you to sign. The contract describes this step as measuring the line rate and obtaining the customer's signature, so you can ask to see that figure before you sign. I wasn't there for the first half of it and a family member signed for me.

Does the Static IP Change After an Upgrade?

It changes, but it's still static.

Logging into the service center, the page said my account details were being updated and a static IP could not be requested, with the whole option greyed out. I assumed I had been dropped back to a dynamic IP. It was only that evening, checking with online chat support, that I found out only the web request form is locked and the public IP is already static. An upgrade counts as an account change, so Chunghwa Telecom issues a new address in place of the old one. If you have an IP hardcoded into firewall rules or DNS records, remember to update it. When in doubt, asking chat support is quicker than guessing.

Speed Test After the Upgrade

fast.com reported 310 Mbps down and 300 Mbps up, with 6 ms unloaded and 9 ms loaded latency.

What the contract quotes is the line rate. Actual throughput varies with your equipment, your surroundings, and whatever you happen to be connecting to, and the service is classed as best effort, so these numbers only describe my line at that moment.

Conclusion

Day to day the upgrade is hard to notice. Web pages and video never needed this much bandwidth in the first place.

Large files are where it shows. Downloads used to sit at 12MB/s no matter what I did, and pulling files from home while I was out was capped by a 5MB/s upload. Neither is true now.

The cost is a couple of hundred more a month, TWD 652.5 up to TWD 899, with points that landed just outside the tier-jump threshold so the 300-point limited-time bonus was all I got, and a mesh unit I never plugged in. For the way I use the line, those two gains are still worth it.

Without that need, a small household that doesn't upload large files often is fine on 100M/40M. By my arithmetic 500M only starts to pay off with around four people sharing it, so I left it alone this time.
