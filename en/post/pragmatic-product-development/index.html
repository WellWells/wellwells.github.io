Mutual vs. Common Knowledge: The Blue-Eyed Islanders Puzzle and Why We Stay Silent

The blue-eyed islanders puzzle and the emperor's new clothes, on mutual versus common knowledge, and why nobody speaks up when everyone in the room already sees the problem.

Introduction

Some phenomena I observed during past projects reminded me of a classic logic puzzle, the "Blue-Eyed Islanders."

On the surface, it looks like a math game. But dig deeper, and it explains something that happens all the time in organizational dynamics: What happens when everyone knows something, but nobody is willing to say it out loud?

This is really about the difference between Mutual Knowledge and Common Knowledge. I've written about the logical side of this in Mutual Knowledge vs. Common Knowledge – How a "Useless" Statement Triggers a Chain Reaction. This article takes a more practical angle, using the blue-eyed puzzle and "The Emperor's New Clothes" story to share some thoughts on group collaboration.

The Blue-Eyed Islanders Puzzle

On an isolated island, there's a tribe of 1,000 people.

The tribe has one strict religious rule: No one is allowed to know their own eye color, and no one is allowed to discuss it. Everyone can see the eye colors of the other 999 people, but there are no mirrors on the island and no one dares break the rule by telling someone their eye color.

If anyone discovers their own eye color, they must perform a ritual suicide at noon the next day in the village square, witnessed by everyone.

All the islanders are perfectly logical and devoutly follow this rule. More importantly, they all know that everyone else is too, and they know that everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on.

Among the 1,000 islanders, 100 have blue eyes and 900 have brown eyes. But the islanders don't know these numbers at the start.

The Tourist's Remark

One day, a tourist visits the island. In front of everyone, he casually says:

"Wow, there are people with blue eyes here!"

To him, it was just an offhand comment. Everyone there could clearly see that blue-eyed people existed on the island.

But on the 100th day after the tourist left, all 100 blue-eyed islanders killed themselves at the same time.

Why? Before you read on, pause and think about it.

Two Seemingly Reasonable Arguments

What makes this puzzle interesting is that there are two arguments that both seem to make sense, yet lead to opposite conclusions:

Argument #1: The tourist's statement had no effect because he didn't tell them anything new. Everyone could already see that there were blue-eyed people.

Argument #2: On the 100th day after the statement, all the blue-eyed people will die.

Which one is right? The answer lies in the difference between Mutual Knowledge and Common Knowledge.

Mutual Knowledge vs. Common Knowledge

To understand this puzzle, we need to get clear on two key concepts.

Mutual Knowledge

  • What it means: Every person in the group knows a certain fact P.
  • Knowledge level: It only goes as deep as "I know P."
  • The catch: Even though A knows P, and B knows P, and maybe A knows that B knows P. Somewhere down the chain, A can't be certain that "everyone knows that everyone already knows this." The chain of "I know that you know that I know..." breaks at some point.

In other words, even if everyone sees the same thing, without some kind of public confirmation, people can't sync up their understanding. So everyone acts on the assumption that others don't know or won't act.

Before the tourist shows up, every blue-eyed person can see that others have blue eyes. But from any villager A's perspective:

  • A knows there are blue-eyed people ✓
  • Does A know that villager B also knows this? Not sure
  • Does A know that B knows that A knows? Even less sure

Common Knowledge

The key difference with common knowledge is that everyone is certain that everyone else knows too.

More specifically, common knowledge has two core features:

  1. Publicness: It is a visceral sense that the fact is "out there," as publicly observable as a physical object.
  2. Instancy: While logically it implies an infinite loop ("I know you know I know..."), in practice, our brains don't run an infinite loop. It is usually triggered instantly by a salient public event (like a broadcast or ritual), snapping everyone's perception into a new synchronized state.

Think of a simple example:

Scenario 1: Whispered Messages

A teacher privately tells each student "there's a test tomorrow," but the students don't know the teacher told anyone else. "There's a test tomorrow" becomes mutual knowledge: every student knows, but nobody knows if others know too.

Scenario 2: Classroom Announcement

The teacher announces "there's a test tomorrow" out loud in front of the whole class. Now it becomes common knowledge. Everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone heard, and everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone heard...

The Key: Common knowledge requires a public announcement, where everyone hears it at the same time and sees that everyone else heard it too. That's why saying things out loud in front of everyone matters so much.

The Difference That Remark Made

When the tourist said "There are blue-eyed people here" in front of everyone:

  • Before: "There are blue-eyed people" was mutual knowledge. Everyone knew, but no one was sure if others knew they knew.
  • After: It became common knowledge. Everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows...

This seemingly tiny change sets off a logical domino effect. A few simple examples make it concrete:

Example 1: 3 People, 1 Blue-Eyed

Say there are only A, B, and C on the island. A has blue eyes, B and C have brown eyes.

  • Before the tourist speaks: A sees B and C have brown eyes, but doesn't know their own eye color. B and C see A has blue eyes, but there's no public confirmation.
  • After the tourist speaks: A sees B and C both have brown eyes, so A immediately realizes "the blue-eyed person must be me" → A dies on Day 1.

Example 2: 4 People, 2 Blue-Eyed

Say there are A, B, C, and D. A and B have blue eyes; C and D have brown eyes.

  • After the tourist speaks:
    • A sees B has blue eyes and thinks: "If I have brown eyes, B is the only one with blue eyes, and B would die on Day 1."
    • B thinks the same about A.
  • Day 1 at noon: Nobody dies.
  • A thinks: "B didn't die, which means B saw another blue-eyed person. That must be me!"
  • B reaches the same conclusion.
  • → A and B both die on Day 2.

Scaling to 100 People

Using mathematical induction, you can prove: If there are n blue-eyed people, they will all die on Day n. Each blue-eyed person waits thinking "if I don't have blue eyes, the others should die by Day n-1." When Day n-1 passes and nobody dies, they realize they must have blue eyes too.

The Emperor's New Clothes

Speaking of public announcements, another classic story is "The Emperor's New Clothes."

In the story, tailors claim they've woven a suit that "only smart people can see." The emperor parades around in clothes that don't exist, and everyone pretends they can see them because no one wants to admit they're "not smart."

Until a child shouts: "The emperor isn't wearing anything!"

That shout is what finally gives everyone permission to admit what they've been seeing all along.

The logic here is exactly the same as the blue-eyed puzzle:

  • Before the child speaks, "the emperor is naked" is mutual knowledge: everyone sees it, but nobody dares confirm that others see it too.
  • The child's words turn it into common knowledge. Everyone finally knows that everyone else saw it too.

The child gave nobody any new information. Everyone could already see the emperor was naked. What he created was common knowledge, and that turned private skepticism into collective ridicule on the spot.

When Silence Becomes the Norm

The reason I wanted to write down these stories and theories is what I saw during past project collaborations.

The Situation

In some past project discussions, I've encountered product directions that were pitched as "breakthroughs." They sounded appealing at first glance, but a closer look often revealed obvious operational gaps:

Overly Idealized Design Mechanisms

Sometimes, in pursuit of extreme control or absolute feature coverage, there is a push to implement overly complex mechanisms. But that usually underestimates the real-world technical costs and compliance challenges, and it underestimates how willing users are to go along with it. The bigger question is whether mechanisms designed for the sake of "completeness" translate into measurable value. That part is hard to prove.

Strategic Transitions Ignoring Market Inertia

There can also be a preference to forcefully adopt trendy strategic transitions, ignoring the established habits of the target market. Copy-pasting a model from an entirely different domain into a specialized context runs into heavy pushback. When the costs paid do not align with the market's perceived value, forcing this kind of transition is an uphill battle.

The Silent Meeting Room

Honestly, I'm not sure if the other participants in those meetings saw the same problems.

But in more rigid organizational structures, internal consensus tends to follow traditional decision-making inertia. When ideas are framed with an aura of "visionary progress," flagging risks can get labeled as resistance to change rather than critical thinking. Nobody wants to be called "uncooperative." In the reality of organizational survival, staying quiet is often the safest move.

So the meeting room vibe became: everyone knows there's a problem, but nobody says anything. That's the trap of mutual knowledge.

This can be explained through avoidance mechanisms: To maintain relationship harmony (e.g., saving face for the person pitching the idea, or avoiding being seen as a troublemaker), we instinctively prevent common knowledge from forming. We keep up a benign hypocrisy, pretending not to see the elephant in the room, and pretending not to know that others are pretending. It is a way of avoiding the moment when a privately known fact becomes a public fact that has to be acted on.

An Outside Voice Breaks the Silence

Then an external partner gave direct feedback. That kind of outside perspective carries no internal position, so it gets through the communication barriers inside an organization and forces the team to face reality.

When the partner said "product development has to be pragmatic," everything clicked.

It reminded me of the blue-eyed puzzle and the emperor's new clothes:

Before anyone spoke up, "this plan has issues" was mutual knowledge: people might have had doubts privately, but avoidance mechanisms kept everyone from confirming if anyone else felt the same.

When an outside voice called it out, it instantly became common knowledge. This solves what Game Theory calls a coordination problem. When we want to align but lack synchronized information, this external voice acts as a prominent Focal Point (or Schelling Point), allowing us to instantly reach a new consensus.

Validation was only half of it. Now that everyone had heard it, we could finally stop pretending.

Conclusion

Turning mutual knowledge into common knowledge matters because only when something gets said out loud can people stop pretending and start dealing with the problem. Everyone knowing something doesn't mean everyone knows that everyone knows, and that small-looking gap changes the outcome completely: one offhand remark sent a hundred people to the village square on day 100, and one child's shout got a whole street to admit the emperor was naked.

The version inside an organization is harder. When decision-making power is unequal, truly objective dialogue is rare, and internal voices get boxed in by role and position, dismissed as not being a team player or lacking the big picture. So sometimes it takes an outside voice to say the thing. The people inside usually knew. They just couldn't say it from where they were standing.

Looking back, I've spent most of my skill points on technical expertise and hard skills and very little on persuasion. Changing someone's mind while challenging an established consensus is genuinely hard. Our partner said product development needs to be pragmatic, and I've come to think team communication does too, except communication takes more nerve.

Humans built complex civilizations not only on reciprocal altruism but on the capacity for common knowledge, which is what makes mutualism possible. Once everyone's understanding is synchronized, a group can push in the same direction without saying a word, the way movers lift a couch together. The elephant in the room works the same way. Nobody wants to be the first to admit it's there.
