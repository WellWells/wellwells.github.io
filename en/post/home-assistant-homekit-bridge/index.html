Hyper-V Home Assistant Setup: Easily Bridge Non-Certified Devices to HomeKit

Setting up Home Assistant on Windows Hyper-V: the external virtual switch and VM settings, then using HomeKit Bridge to get AdGuard Home, Synology NAS, and IR appliances into the Apple Home app.

Introduction

I've always admired the polish of Apple's HomeKit ecosystem. With a HomePod mini in the house, whether I'm streaming music or controlling natively supported smart accessories, the response time and stability have both kept me happy.

But the cracks show fast.

A few of my TP-Link smart plugs don't support HomeKit. Meaning I have to open a separate app just to toggle them. My old infrared air conditioner is stuck in its own app. The Android TV box in the living room can't even be turned on remotely. Three different apps for three devices in the same room.

At some point I got fed up and used some spare time to set up Home Assistant (HA) and see if I could fix this.

How Smart Homes Work: Why We Need Home Assistant

The short version: your phone speaks Wi-Fi, but a bunch of smart home devices don't. Why? Battery life.

Devices plugged into the wall—like smart plugs or light bulbs—can afford the power draw of Wi-Fi. But a battery-powered door sensor or wireless switch running on a coin cell? Wi-Fi would kill the battery in days. That's why most of these smaller gadgets run on:

  • Zigbee: Extremely low-power, short-range, good for sensors and switches.
  • Thread: Similar idea but newer. It's the standard Apple and Google have been pushing. Devices can also relay signals through each other like a mesh, which helps range.

Your router doesn't speak either of those protocols. So your network needs a physical bridge (gateway): a device that stays powered on and translates between the low-power radio signals and your local network.

Big Tech's Walled Gardens and the Reality of Matter

To lock you into their ecosystems, major brands traditionally required you to buy their specific proprietary hubs. If a device didn't natively support HomeKit, it remained trapped inside its own app unless you bought yet another bridge.

The industry tried to fix this with the Matter protocol, a universal standard backed by Apple, Google, Amazon, and others. On paper, Matter means one device works everywhere. In practice, it's been a slow rollout. Brands like Philips Hue and IKEA have been dragging their feet on full support, and older hardware either needs firmware updates or just won't qualify at all. You still end up buying proprietary hubs if you want everything to work together.

Home Assistant is where I landed instead. It connects to basically anything—official brand hubs, direct USB radio dongles, local network APIs—and pulls it all into one dashboard. Getting my Synology NAS stats in there was as simple as searching for the integration and entering the IP and credentials.

A Low-Cost, 24/7 Solution

Since HA needs to run 24/7, the question is what machine it runs on.

A dedicated HA mini PC runs around TWD 3,000. My desktop already stays on all the time as a local server, so I just carved out a small chunk of resources and ran HA inside a Windows Hyper-V virtual machine. Zero extra cost.

One thing worth knowing: if you want remote control via the Apple Home app when you're away from home, you still need a HomePod mini or Apple TV on your network. HA handles the local translation of non-certified devices, but Apple's infrastructure is what actually bridges the gap to your phone on cellular. That's just how HomeKit works, HA or not.

If you don't care about Apple Home and want to use the native HA app for remote access, you'll need to handle that yourself. Don't just open a port on your router. That's asking for trouble. The cleaner option is Tailscale. Install the Tailscale add-on in HA, and you get an encrypted VPN tunnel between your phone and the VM without touching your router config. When you're out, turn on Tailscale and HA is right there.

Here's how I set the whole thing up, from VM creation to getting AdGuard Home and NAS monitoring showing up in iOS.

Four Steps to Setting Up Home Assistant on Hyper-V

If this is your first time using a VM, here's the full process:

Step 1: Prerequisites and Downloading Files

  1. Enable Hyper-V: Turn on the Hyper-V virtualization feature in Windows 10/11 (Pro or Enterprise).
  2. Download the Image: Head to the Official Home Assistant Installation Page, download the Hyper-V (.vhdx) file, and extract it.
  3. Set the Storage Path: Move the .vhdx file to a permanent directory (e.g., C:\Users\wells\HomeAssistant). Do not move or delete this file later, or your virtual machine will fail to boot and cause data loss.

Step 2: Create an "External Virtual Switch"

This is what gets the virtual machine an IP on the same subnet as your router (e.g., 192.168.x.x), so it can talk directly to the other smart devices on your network.

  1. Open Hyper-V Manager and select Virtual Switch Manager from the Actions panel on the right.
  2. Select New virtual network switch -> choose External -> click Create Virtual Switch.
  3. Configure the Properties:
    • Name: Enter HA-External-Switch.
    • Connection type: Select External network and use the dropdown to choose the network adapter your computer currently uses for internet access.
    • Crucial Checkbox: Check Allow management operating system to share this network adapter, or your host Windows machine loses its internet connection.
  4. Click OK. If a warning pops up regarding temporary network disruption, click Yes.

Step 3: Create the Virtual Machine and Optimize Performance

This step deploys the virtual machine with "Quick Create" and sorts out its resource allocation in one go.

  1. Click Quick Create on the right side of the Hyper-V Manager:

    • Select an image: Choose Local installation source and browse to the .vhdx file you prepared in Step 1.
    • Advanced settings: Click More options in the bottom right, name the VM HA or Home Assistant, and set the network to the HA-External-Switch created in Step 2.
    • Security: You must uncheck "Enable Windows Secure Boot."

  2. Click Create Virtual Machine. Once created, do not connect to it just yet. Click Edit Settings in the confirmation window and adjust the resources:

    • Memory: Set it to 4096 MB (4 GB) and uncheck "Enable Dynamic Memory" to prevent resource contention.
    • Processor: Set the number of virtual processors to 2.

  3. Click Apply and OK to save your changes.

Step 4: Boot the Virtual Machine and Initialize the System

With the resources set, it's time to fire up Home Assistant OS.

  1. Back in the main Hyper-V Manager interface, right-click your newly created VM and select Connect. In the console window that pops up, click Start.

  2. A stream of boot logs will scroll past as the Linux subsystem initializes. Wait about 1 to 3 minutes. When the welcome banner appears, your system is officially live on your local network:

  3. Open a web browser on any device connected to the same local network and navigate to: http://homeassistant.local:8123 (or use the explicit IP shown on screen, e.g., http://192.168.1.125:8123).

  4. The system will download core components in the background. Once the onboarding screen appears, follow the prompts to create your admin account and set your location. That completes the Hyper-V side of the setup.

Device Integration: Adding Hardware to Your Dashboard

With the system up, next is adding devices to the Home Assistant dashboard.

Click the + icon in the top right corner of the web UI and select Add Device.

To integrate an AdGuard Home instance, type adguard into the search bar. Select the integration, enter your host IP/URL, port, and admin credentials. Once submitted, your AdGuard ad-blocking toggles and real-time statistics will populate right into HA.

The same approach works for Synology DSM. Enter your NAS's local IP and credentials, and HA pulls the server's hardware status and system metrics in automatically.

Syncing HA Devices to Apple Home

Getting your HA devices into Apple Home is actually pretty easy. Two things: set up a bridge in HA, then scan a QR code with your iPhone.

Create a HomeKit Bridge in HA

  1. Click the + icon in the top right of HA (Add Device), search for apple, and select Apple.
  2. Choose HomeKit Bridge. In the configuration window, check the device domains you want to expose to Apple (such as Switch or Sensor) and hit submit. (Note: Media players and TVs will automatically be isolated into "accessory mode" so they get dedicated control interfaces within iOS).

iPhone QR Code Pairing

  1. Once the bridge is created, click the Notifications (bell icon) in the bottom-left corner of HA. You will see a dedicated setup QR code alongside an 8-digit PIN (standalone accessories like TVs will also be displayed separately here).
  2. Open the Home app on your iPhone, tap the + icon in the top right, select Add Accessory, and scan the QR code off your screen.
  3. When the prompt detects the bridge, tap Add to Home and assign your devices to their respective rooms.

Once paired, look at your iOS Home app. Peripherals that previously locked you out of HomeKit—like your TV or AdGuard switches—are all in place. Siri voice commands, automation triggers, or checking the NAS drive temperature from an Apple Watch all work from there. (Note: The Home app UI only showcases the AdGuard Home switch controlled via HA).

Conclusion

After setting up the HomeKit Bridge, I can now pull most of my non-HomeKit hardware into the Apple Home app without buying new devices.

Before HA, buying smart home gear meant filtering for that "Works with Apple HomeKit" badge, which pretty much always comes with a price premium. Now I just check if something has a Home Assistant integration. That's a way broader pool of hardware, and a lot of it is cheaper. My IR-controlled appliances that were trapped in proprietary apps are now controllable through Apple Home.

For new purchases, I still lean toward devices that natively support Matter: it's the least-headache option long term and works across both iOS and Android without needing HA in the loop. But for everything already sitting in my home, HA bridged the gap pretty cleanly.
