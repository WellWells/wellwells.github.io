M.2 refers to a small, efficient interface standard that supports multiple signal protocols. For SSDs, the protocols are SATA and PCI Express (PCIe).

  • SATA: Maximum speed of 6 Gbps, with a practical limit of 600 MB/s. Prefer NVMe over SATA to get the full 1000 MB/s of USB 3.2 Gen 2.
  • NVMe: A protocol designed for PCIe-based flash memory, marked as PCIe Gen3 or PCIe Gen4 during SSD selection. PCIe Gen 4 offers faster speeds, but for external SSDs using USB Gen 2, PCIe Gen 3 is sufficient, achieving around 1000 MB/s.

This article focuses on choosing NVMe M.2 SSDs + external enclosures, highlighting the key parameters to consider.

Priority Order for Drive Selection

  • Portability: Flash Drive > SSD > HDD
  • Performance: SSD > Flash Drive > HDD
  • Large Capacity Consideration: HDD > SSD > Flash Drive
  • Cost: Flash Drive > HDD > SSD
  • OS Boot: SSD > HDD > Flash Drive
  • Durability: Flash Drive > SSD >> HDD
  • Power Consumption: SSD > Flash Drive > HDD
  • If Money is No Object: SSD > Flash Drive > HDD

USB External Interface Specifications

Regardless of the protocol or technology used internally in an external hard drive, communication with the host or other devices is ultimately conducted via the USB interface. The USB specifications have undergone several revisions, and the naming and specifications of each version can be confusing. The commonly used USB versions at present include:

  1. USB 3.2 Gen 1: Maximum transfer speed of approximately 500 MB/s.
  2. USB 3.2 Gen 2: Maximum transfer speed of approximately 1200 MB/s.
  3. USB 3.2 Gen 2×2: Maximum transfer speed of approximately 2400 MB/s.

NVMe M.2 SSD Purchase Considerations

TLC or QLC?

When selecting an SSD (Solid-State Drive), TLC (Triple-Level Cell) is generally the preferred option over QLC (Quad-Level Cell). QLC is not the mainstream choice in the current market, especially for applications requiring high durability and stability.

Common types of SSDs include TLC (storing 3 bits per cell), QLC (storing 4 bits per cell), and occasionally MLC (Multi-Level Cell, storing 2 bits per cell).

  • Relationship between Bit Count and Number of Cells: The fewer bits stored per cell, the more cells are required to achieve the same capacity. For example, MLC (2 bits) requires more cells than TLC (3 bits) to reach the same storage capacity.
  • Durability and Lifespan: With more cells available, data can have greater redundancy to prevent read/write failures, resulting in a longer lifespan. Since MLC stores fewer bits per cell, it generally has higher durability due to the greater number of cells used for the same capacity.
  • Data Processing Speed: Processing cells with fewer bits is relatively faster during data read/write operations.

PCIe Gen 3 or PCIe Gen 4?

Most external enclosures on the market are equipped with a controller chip that supports PCIe Gen 3, paired with USB 3.2 Gen 2 transfer speeds (theoretical value of approximately 10 Gbps, with actual transfer speeds around 1000–1200 MB/s). So for general use, PCIe Gen 3 with read/write bandwidth above 1200 MB/s is enough.

For assembling advanced USB 3.2 Gen 2×2 systems to achieve faster transfer speeds, you need to verify that the enclosure's controller chip supports this standard and that the chosen NVMe M.2 SSD has read/write performance greater than 2400 MB/s.

Is DRAM Necessary?

When selecting an NVMe M.2 SSD, go for models with at least 1 GB of DRAM and avoid DRAMless versions.

DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory) acts as a cache in NVMe M.2 SSDs, lifting data transfer efficiency, with the biggest difference showing up in random read/write operations. Its primary function is to store the mapping table that records the actual storage locations of data in the NAND flash memory. With DRAM, the SSD can quickly locate and access data, and response times drop.

To reduce costs, some NVMe M.2 SSDs do not include an additional DRAM chip, and these products are usually labeled as "DRAMless". SSDs without independent DRAM rely on two alternative methods to store the mapping table and cache data:

  1. NAND Flash Memory Itself: Directly storing the mapping table in NAND, though NAND's access speed is much lower than that of DRAM, leading to performance degradation.
  2. Host Memory (the computer's DRAM): Through Host Memory Buffer (HMB) technology, the SSD can directly access the computer's DRAM via the PCIe interface as a cache.

External hard drives use the USB protocol, and HMB only works over PCIe. So in USB external applications, a DRAMless SSD cannot fall back on host memory to make up the difference.

Choosing External Enclosure Controller Chips?

When selecting an external hard drive enclosure controller chip, the primary consideration is the protocol supported by the M.2 slot, which can be either SATA or PCIe (NVMe). The controller chip is responsible for bridging PCIe or SATA and converting it to a USB interface. This directly affects the USB 3.2 speed supported by the external hard drive enclosure. For USB 3.2 Gen 2×2, the SSD's read/write speeds must exceed 2400 MB/s to actually reach that speed.

Some products may use the Intel JHL7440 chip, which is relatively outdated by today's standards. In such cases, the ASM2464PD chip can be chosen as a substitute.

Sliding Cover or Clamshell Enclosure?

Prefer clamshell over sliding cover enclosures.

Many enclosures marketed as tool-free are sliding cover types, where the M.2 SSD can be removed like a drawer. Sliding cover enclosures often have independent heat sinks that do not effectively conduct heat to the case. Conversely, clamshell enclosures do not slide, allowing direct heat transfer from the heat sink to the case. More reviews can be found online for informed choices.

Normal Operating Temperature for SSDs?

For desktop use, SSD temperatures are typically around 42 °C, with normal operating temperatures between 50 °C and 55 °C. Exceeding 60 °C can reduce speed and lifespan.

Formatting Choices for Drives?

exFAT is the preferred choice for cross-device compatibility.

Choosing exFAT allows for data transfer across devices (Windows, macOS, iOS, Android) with smooth reading and writing.

For specific device use, choose the corresponding format:

  • Windows: NTFS
  • macOS / iPad / iOS: AFPS
  • Android: exFAT

Not Achieving Expected Speeds?

Check cable length, USB port support, controller chip support for PCIe, controller chip firmware, and SSD cache.

Speed is the thing people focus on when building or buying a fast SSD. You already spent the money, so falling short of the speed you expected stings.

To troubleshoot speed issues, check:

  1. Is the cable shorter than 0.8 m?
  2. Does the computer's USB port support the required USB version?
  3. Is the Type C to Type A adapter faulty?
  4. Does the M.2 SSD's read/write speed meet USB's speed (PCIe read/write only 2000 MB/s cannot reach USB4 speeds)?
  5. Performance of the controller chip
  6. Is the controller chip firmware up-to-date?
  7. Is the SSD cache full, causing high temperatures and reduced speeds?
  8. Is write caching enabled?

Conclusion

Practical application, capacity, cost, heat dissipation, and brand reputation are what usually settle which SSD you end up with. Look up online read/write benchmarks before deciding.

If you don't have the energy to evaluate M.2 SSDs, picking a widely bought model from a major manufacturer is less likely to go wrong.

For example, the external SSD products commonly seen in the market in 2025 include:

  1. ADATA SD810
  2. ADATA SE880
  3. ADATA SE900G
  4. Crucial X9 Pro
  5. Crucial X10 Pro
  6. Kingston XS1000
  7. Kingston XS2000
  8. SanDisk Extreme PRO Portable SSD V2
  9. SK hynix Beetle X31
  10. TeamGroup T-Force M200

These external SSDs do well on design and protection, but the compact size means thermal management gets compromised.

Some of these are USB 3.2 Gen 2 (10 Gbps) class, including the Crucial X9 Pro, Kingston XS1000, and SK hynix Beetle X31, and only the rest are Gen 2×2 (20 Gbps). On top of that, differences in NVMe controller chips and cache configurations mean real-world speeds vary.

Read real-world performance reviews before buying, so you know whether the drive fits what you need. The only certainty is that the prices for these end products are relatively high.
